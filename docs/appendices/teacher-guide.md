# Appendix C: Teacher Guide

This guide helps you plan with *Gut Check*. It has four pacing options, a one-row-per-chapter teaching table, a Hype Meter activity you can run in any class, assessment ideas, and notes on differentiation and accessibility. For an overview of the book, licensing, and embedding sims, start with [For Teachers](../teachers.md). Before teaching, read the [notes on teaching sensitive topics](../teachers.md#teaching-sensitive-topics).

All times assume class periods of about 50 minutes. Every MicroSim page has its own Lesson Plan with timing, steps, and assessment questions, so check it before class.

## Hype Meter classroom activity

**Four Corners: Grade the Claim** (about 25 minutes, any course, after Chapter 1 or any later chapter)

**Goal:** Students practice grading claims by the evidence behind them, not by how confident they sound.

**Setup:** Label the four corners of the room **Strong**, **Moderate**, **Preliminary**, and **Hype**. Post the [Hype Meter](../hype-meter.md) definitions and the six questions where everyone can see them.

**Steps:**

1. **Read a claim (1 minute).** Read one claim aloud. Students walk to the corner that matches their grade. (For students who can't move easily, use colored cards held up at desks instead.)
2. **Defend it (3 minutes).** Each corner gets 30 seconds to explain its choice. Push for evidence words: mice or people, how many, correlation or cause.
3. **Reveal (1 minute).** Share the book's grade and the reason. Students may switch corners and say what changed their mind.
4. **Repeat** with four or five claims.
5. **Wrap up (5 minutes).** Each student writes one sentence: "A claim moves up the Hype Meter when..."

**Starter claims, with the book's grades** (from [The Hype Meter](../hype-meter.md) page and the chapter cards):

| Claim | Book's grade |
|---|---|
| "The gut has its own network of neurons that can run reflexes without the brain." | Strong |
| "Eating lots of ultra-processed food is linked to depression." | Moderate (a large, repeated link, but the cause is unclear) |
| "People with IBD have higher rates of anxiety and depression symptoms." | Strong (as an association; it does not show cause) |
| "Probiotics treat depression." | Preliminary |
| "70% of your immune system lives in your gut." | Hype |
| "Your gut is a second brain that thinks and feels." | Hype |

Add claims from the Hype Meter cards in the chapters you've taught. Each card states its verdict and "what would change the grade." For a harder round, have students rewrite each Hype claim into a careful version that would earn a higher grade.

**Follow-up:** The [Hype Meter Sorter](../sims/ch20-hype-meter-sorter/index.md) gives each student individual practice and tracks whether they grade "too generous" or "too harsh."

## Assessment ideas

### Quizzes

Each chapter has a 10-question multiple-choice quiz with explanations on its own page. Use two or three questions as an exit ticket, or the whole quiz as a check at the end of a chapter. Because the answers are on the same page, copy the questions into your own form if you want a closed-book quiz (credit the book, as the license requires).

### Your Gut Check

Each chapter's Your Gut Check has five parts: a Claim Check, three scenarios (Predict, Explain, Apply), and a Read-the-data question. Ideas:

- **Warm-up:** project the Claim Check and have students commit to a grade before opening the answer.
- **Homework:** assign the three scenarios, then compare student answers with the model answers in class.
- **Data skills check:** the Read-the-data question works as a short graded task. Ask what the data show, what they can't show, and one follow-up study.

### Mock exam

[Appendix A: Mock Exam](mock-exam.md) is a cumulative review. Use the full exam at the end of a semester, or pick questions that match the chapters you taught for a shorter unit test.

### The Chapter 20 student research project

The project in [Section 20.14](../chapters/20-be-your-own-scientist/index.md#2014-your-turn-a-student-research-project) asks each student to pick one real health claim, find the original evidence, fill in the six-question table, assign a Hype Meter grade, and write a plain-language summary. It works as a capstone for any of the pacing options.

**Tips:**

- Have students choose claims from public sources (ads, headlines, or posts by public accounts), not from classmates or family.
- Steer students away from claims about their own health or diagnosis, unless they choose that freely.
- Check claims early. Some popular claims have no original study at all, which is a finding worth reporting.

**A simple rubric (4 points each, 20 total):**

| Criterion | 4: Strong work | 2: Partly there | 0: Missing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Claim and question** | Claim copied exactly with source; question names people, cause, outcome, and comparison | Claim or question is vague | Missing |
| **Sources** | At least three, including the original study (or a note that none exists) and a trusted plain-language source, all with working links | Fewer sources or weak ones | Missing |
| **Six-question table and numbers** | All six questions answered from the study; numbers reported carefully | Some questions guessed or skipped | Missing |
| **Grade and reasoning** | Grade fits the evidence; says what study would change it | Grade given with thin reasoning | Missing |
| **Plain-language summary and safety** | Clear for a 9th grader; states limits; includes "talk to your doctor" | Some jargon or missing limits | Missing |

### Other options

- **At the Bedside write-up:** students explain the case's "Connecting the signals" reasoning in their own words, then answer the "Your turn" question.
- **Exercises:** each chapter has 12 exercises, two at each Bloom's level. The Evaluate and Create questions make good short projects.
- **MicroSim lab report:** students record a prediction, data from the sim, and one limit from the "What this model leaves out" box.

## Differentiation and accessibility

### For students who need more support

- Start each chapter with the hook and the everyday comparison, read aloud together.
- Pre-teach the bolded terms, and use the [glossary](../glossary.md) and the [learning graph](../sims/graph-viewer/index.md).
- Give a partly filled six-question table for the "How to read a gut-brain claim" box.
- Assign the Remember and Understand exercises first, and use the sim in pairs with clear roles (one runs, one records).
- Read quiz questions aloud for students who benefit from it.

### For students ready for more

- Assign the Evaluate and Create exercises, or the full student research project early.
- Ask students to find one real study cited in a chapter's References page and fill in their own six-question table.
- Have students design a new MicroSim variable on paper and predict what it would show.
- Advanced students can explore the prerequisites from book #1, [*From Neuron to Bedside*](https://aaryavvatts-lab.github.io/neuroscience-textbook/).

### English learners

- The book uses short sentences and defines each term in plain words before the technical name.
- Let students write predictions and explanations in their first language first, then in English.
- Browser translation tools work on the text pages. Check translations of key terms with the glossary.

### Accessibility

- **Devices.** The book and sims work on phones, tablets, Chromebooks, and laptops.
- **Screen readers.** Each sim has a short text description, but it doesn't announce every change. Pair students who use screen readers with a partner, and use each sim's "About This MicroSim" section.
- **Movement.** Offer a seated version of any activity that asks students to move around the room.
- **Printing.** Printable PDFs are on the [Downloads](../downloads.md) page for students with limited internet at home.
- **Health needs.** Some students may have a gut condition and need to leave class quickly. Allow it without questions, and never make them explain. Chapter 19 covers school accommodations such as 504 plans.

### Keep it safe

Never ask students to share their own symptoms, diets, or mental health, and avoid food-shaming. If a student seems to be struggling, connect them with the school counselor. In the US, anyone can call or text **988** to reach the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. Nothing in this book is medical advice; for any personal health question, the answer is "talk to your doctor."
