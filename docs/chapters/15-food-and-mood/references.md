# References: Food and Mood

1. [A randomised controlled trial of dietary improvement for adults with major depression (the "SMILES" trial)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5282719/). Jacka FN, et al. *BMC Medicine*, 2017. The trial analyzed in this chapter's claim box: 67 adults with depression, 12 weeks, remission 32.3% vs 8.0%. The free full text explains the diet, the blinding, and the funding. (A correction was published in 2018.)

2. [The Effects of Dietary Improvement on Symptoms of Depression and Anxiety: A Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6455094/). Firth J, et al. *Psychosomatic Medicine*, 2019. Combines 16 trials with 45,826 participants and finds a small effect on depressive symptoms and no significant effect on anxiety. Useful for seeing how one exciting trial fits into the bigger picture.

3. [Mediterranean dietary pattern and depression: the PREDIMED randomized trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3848350/). Sánchez-Villegas A, et al. *BMC Medicine*, 2013. A large trial in older Spanish adults at high heart risk that found no significant overall effect on new depression. A good reminder that bigger trials sometimes give more modest answers.

4. [Ultra-Processed Food Consumption and Mental Health: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Observational Studies](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9268228/). Lane MM, et al. *Nutrients*, 2022. Pools 17 observational studies of 385,541 people, explains the NOVA classification, and notes that the link appears to run in both directions.

5. [Ultra-processed food exposure and adverse health outcomes: umbrella review of epidemiological meta-analyses](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10899807/). Lane MM, et al. *BMJ*, 2024. The umbrella review covering nearly 10 million people. Shows how evidence can be "convincing" as an association yet "low" in quality.

6. [Food and mood: how do diet and nutrition affect mental wellbeing?](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7322666/). Firth J, Gangwisch JE, Borisini A, Wootton RE, Mayer EA. *BMJ*, 2020. A readable overview of nutritional psychiatry and the possible pathways, including the gut microbiome, with an honest discussion of what is still unknown.

7. [Gut-microbiota-targeted diets modulate human immune status](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9020749/). Wastyk HC, et al. *Cell*, 2021. The Stanford trial in which 36 healthy adults ate more fermented foods or more fiber for 17 weeks; the fermented-food group had higher microbe diversity and lower inflammation markers. It did not measure mood.

8. [Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2020-2025](https://www.dietaryguidelines.gov/sites/default/files/2020-12/Dietary_Guidelines_for_Americans_2020-2025.pdf). US Department of Agriculture and US Department of Health and Human Services. Source for the teen fiber amounts (about 25 g for girls and 31 g for boys aged 14 to 18) and the share of Americans who don't meet them.

9. [Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2025-2030](https://cdn.realfood.gov/DGA.pdf). US Department of Agriculture and US Department of Health and Human Services. The current federal guidelines. Their "Gut Health" box says fermented and high-fiber foods support a diverse microbiome; they give no gram target for fiber.

10. [Food Security in the U.S.: Key Statistics and Graphics](https://www.ers.usda.gov/topics/food-nutrition-assistance/food-security-in-the-us/key-statistics-graphics). USDA Economic Research Service. Official 2024 numbers on food insecurity (13.7% of households; 18.4% of households with children) and the definition used in this chapter.

11. [Eating Disorders](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/topics/eating-disorders). National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH). Explains what eating disorders are, that they are serious, and that they can be treated successfully, with early help being important. A trustworthy place to start if food worries are affecting you or someone you know.
