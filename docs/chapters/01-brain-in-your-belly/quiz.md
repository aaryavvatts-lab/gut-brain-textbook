#### Question 1

Where is the myenteric plexus located?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. In the mucosa, right under the cells that touch food
2. Between the circular and longitudinal muscle layers of the gut wall
3. In the brainstem, where the vagus nerve begins
4. In the serosa, on the outside surface of the gut
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The myenteric (Auerbach's) plexus sits between the two smooth muscle sheets and mainly controls gut movement. Option A describes roughly where the submucosal plexus is (just under the lining, in the submucosa), which is an easy mix-up.

    **Concept Tested:** Myenteric Plexus
    **Bloom's Level:** Remember

#### Question 2

Which statement about the number of neurons in the human enteric nervous system matches the evidence in this chapter?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It has about 86 billion neurons, the same as the brain.
2. It has more neurons than the brain, which is why it is called the second brain.
3. It has only a few thousand neurons, too few to run reflexes.
4. It has hundreds of millions of neurons, about 400 to 600 million by common estimates.
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Common estimates put the ENS at about 400 to 600 million neurons, roughly as many as the spinal cord. About 86 billion is the brain's count, so options A and B confuse the two; the brain has well over a hundred times more neurons than the gut.

    **Concept Tested:** ENS Neuron Count
    **Bloom's Level:** Remember

#### Question 3

What are interstitial cells of Cajal best known for?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Generating the slow electrical waves that set the timing of gut muscle contractions
2. Sensing stretch and triggering the peristaltic reflex by themselves
3. Making most of the body's antibodies in the gut lining
4. Carrying signals from the gut up the vagus nerve to the brain
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. ICC are the gut's pacemaker cells: they generate slow waves that time smooth muscle contractions. Option B is tempting, but stretch is sensed by enteric sensory neurons, and animals without an ENS can't push food along even when ICC are present.

    **Concept Tested:** Interstitial Cells of Cajal
    **Bloom's Level:** Remember

#### Question 4

Why does the muscle need to relax *ahead* of a ball of food during the peristaltic reflex?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Relaxation ahead signals the brain that food has arrived.
2. Relaxation ahead lets the pacemaker cells reset their rhythm.
3. Relaxation ahead opens the tube so the squeeze behind can actually move the food forward.
4. Relaxation ahead is not needed; contraction behind the food is enough.
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Contraction behind the food only pushes it forward if the segment ahead opens up. Option D is the key misconception: if the segment ahead stays contracted, as in Hirschsprung disease, squeezing behind just builds pressure and the food stalls.

    **Concept Tested:** Peristaltic Reflex
    **Bloom's Level:** Understand

#### Question 5

In Hirschsprung disease, what happens to the segment of bowel that has no enteric neurons?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It goes limp and floppy, so stool slides through too fast.
2. It stays contracted and cannot relax, so stool backs up behind it.
3. It becomes inflamed because immune cells attack the neurons.
4. It is controlled directly by the vagus nerve instead, so it works normally.
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Without enteric neurons, there are no inhibitory neurons to relax the muscle, so the segment stays contracted and causes a functional blockage. Option A is a common guess, but losing the neurons removes the "relax" signal, not the muscle's tone.

    **Concept Tested:** Hirschsprung Disease
    **Bloom's Level:** Understand

#### Question 6

Why is the end of the bowel the part left without neurons when ENS development falls short?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Neural crest cells enter the top of the gut and migrate downward, so the far end is colonized last.
2. The end of the bowel destroys neural crest cells as they arrive.
3. Neurons at the end of the bowel are made by the brain and arrive after birth.
4. The end of the bowel does not need neurons in healthy people.
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Most enteric neurons come from the vagal neural crest, enter the top of the gut, and colonize it from top to bottom, so a migration that stops short leaves the far end empty. Option D is wrong because Hirschsprung disease shows that the end of the bowel does need its neurons.

    **Concept Tested:** Neural Crest
    **Bloom's Level:** Understand

#### Question 7

A post says: "Your gut is a second brain that thinks and feels." Using the Hype Meter, what grade does this claim as stated deserve?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Strong, because the gut really has its own nervous system
2. Moderate, because the gut has many neurons but scientists are unsure what they do
3. Preliminary, because only mouse studies have looked at gut neurons
4. Hype, because the ENS runs reflexes, and no evidence shows it thinks or feels
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The Hype Meter grades the claim as stated. The structure fact is Strong, but the leap to "thinks and feels" is not supported: the research describes reflexes for movement, secretion, and blood flow. Option A grades the true starting fact instead of the actual claim.

    **Concept Tested:** Hype Meter
    **Bloom's Level:** Apply

#### Question 8

Riya's friend learns that after a vagotomy (cutting the vagus nerve), the stomach part of the migrating motor complex stops but the small intestine's repeating activity continues. What is the best conclusion?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The vagus nerve controls all gut movement, so the small intestine must be getting signals another way.
2. The migrating motor complex happens only after meals.
3. The small intestine can keep its between-meal contraction pattern without the vagus nerve, which fits local control by the ENS.
4. The stomach has more enteric neurons than the small intestine.
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The small intestine keeps its own schedule without the vagus nerve, which supports the idea that the enteric nervous system can run gut patterns locally. Option A assumes the brain must be in charge, which is exactly what the result argues against; option B is wrong because the MMC happens during fasting and is interrupted by eating.

    **Concept Tested:** Migrating Motor Complex
    **Bloom's Level:** Apply

#### Question 9

The 2020 enteric neuron atlas profiled colon tissue from 16 adults having colon cancer surgery and recovered 1,445 human neurons. Which limitation matters most if you want to apply its human findings to healthy teens?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The study was paid for by a supplement company.
2. The human tissue came from a small number of adults with cancer, who may differ from healthy teens.
3. The study used only mice, so it says nothing about humans.
4. The study was a randomized trial, so it can't describe cell types.
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The human samples came from 16 adults aged 35 to 90 having cancer surgery, so they may not represent healthy young people. Option C is wrong because the study included human tissue, and option A is wrong because its funders were mainly research institutes, foundations, and NIH grants.

    **Concept Tested:** Evidence Grade
    **Bloom's Level:** Analyze

#### Question 10

In 1994, mice with mutations in the gene for Kit had very few interstitial cells of Cajal and no slow waves, while their normal siblings had slow waves. What does this experiment show, and what is its main limit?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It shows only a correlation in humans, and its limit is that it was too large.
2. It proves that the brain sets the gut's rhythm, and its limit is that it was short.
3. It shows that enteric neurons are not needed for movement, and its limit is funding.
4. It tests cause (change one gene, lose the cells, lose the rhythm), and its limit is that it was done in mice.
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Changing one gene and seeing the cells and rhythm disappear is an experiment that points to cause, but it was done in mice, so it needed human evidence before the claim could be graded Strong. Option C overreaches: the mutant mice's muscles still responded to enteric nerves, and other work shows the ENS is needed for propulsion.

    **Concept Tested:** Interstitial Cells of Cajal
    **Bloom's Level:** Analyze
