# Chapter 14: Parkinson's, Alzheimer's, and the Gut

## Summary

Parkinson's disease is best known for tremor and slow movement, but many people have gut problems, especially constipation, years before any movement symptoms appear. This chapter explains how Parkinson's damages dopamine neurons, how a misfolded protein called alpha-synuclein may spread from cell to cell, and why some scientists think the disease can sometimes begin in the gut and travel up the vagus nerve. You will weigh the evidence from autopsy studies, mouse experiments, and large health-record studies of people who had their vagus nerve cut or their appendix removed, and you will compare the "gut-first" and "brain-first" ideas side by side. The chapter ends with Alzheimer's disease, where gut microbe research is much younger, and with practical points about how the gut affects Parkinson's medicine.

## 14.1 Riya's Grandfather and a Clue From Years Ago

Riya's grandfather, whom she calls Nanaji, is 72. Two years ago his right hand started shaking when it rested in his lap, and his walk turned into small, shuffling steps. His neurologist diagnosed Parkinson's disease. At a family dinner, Riya's grandmother mentioned something odd: "His stomach trouble started long before the shaking. He was constipated for years, and he stopped being able to smell my cooking." Riya, who had just learned in biology club that the gut has its own nervous system, sat up. Could a brain disease have started in the gut? (Riya and her family are fictional and appear throughout this book.)

Riya's question is a real one that scientists are arguing about right now. Her grandmother's observation matches a pattern doctors have noticed in many people with Parkinson's: gut problems, smell loss, and certain sleep problems often show up years before any shaking. This chapter follows that clue. We'll see where the evidence is strong, where it is mixed, and where it turns into hype.

Here's the plan. First, what goes wrong in the brain in Parkinson's. Then the early clues, the idea that the disease can travel from gut to brain, and the experiments that test it in mice and people. Finally, Alzheimer's disease, where the gut story is younger and less certain.

## 14.2 When Brain Cells Wear Out: Neurodegeneration

Picture a row of old houses on a street. Most stay standing for a century. But in one neighborhood, the houses start to fall apart one after another, and the damage seems to move from one house to its neighbors. That's a rough picture of what happens in some brain diseases.

**Neurodegeneration** is the slow, progressive loss of the structure and function of neurons, ending in their death, in particular parts of the nervous system. "Progressive" means it gets worse over time. Neurons in the adult brain are mostly not replaced when they die, so the loss adds up. Parkinson's disease and Alzheimer's disease are the two most common neurodegenerative diseases, and both mostly affect older adults.

You already know the basics of neurons and how they signal to each other. If you'd like a deeper review of how the aging brain changes and how these diseases affect it, see [From Neuron to Bedside, Chapter 13](https://aaryavvatts-lab.github.io/neuroscience-textbook/chapters/13-aging-and-neurodegeneration/). For how the brain controls movement, see [From Neuron to Bedside, Chapter 7](https://aaryavvatts-lab.github.io/neuroscience-textbook/chapters/07-movement/).

## 14.3 Parkinson's Disease: Losing the Dopamine Neurons

**Parkinson's disease** (PD) is a progressive disorder of the nervous system that mainly affects movement, caused by the gradual damage and death of nerve cells in certain parts of the brain. [The National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke (NINDS)](https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/disorders/parkinsons-disease) lists four common movement symptoms:

- **Tremor:** rhythmic shaking, often starting in one hand and most obvious when the hand is at rest. Some people with PD never develop a tremor.
- **Rigidity:** muscles that stay tight and stiff.
- **Bradykinesia:** slowness of movement, so everyday tasks like dressing take longer, and the face may show less expression.
- **Postural instability:** balance problems that raise the risk of falls.

Why these symptoms? Deep in the brain is a small region called the substantia nigra ("black substance," because its cells look dark). Its neurons make dopamine, a chemical messenger that helps the brain produce smooth, purposeful movements. **Dopamine neuron loss** is the death of these dopamine-making neurons in the substantia nigra, the main cause of the movement symptoms of Parkinson's. According to NINDS, by the time movement symptoms appear, most people have already lost 60 to 80% or more of these cells.

Read that number again. The brain has a lot of backup capacity, so it can lose most of these neurons before anything looks wrong from the outside. That means the disease process has been running quietly for a long time before diagnosis. This is the window where the gut clues live.

Parkinson's is not only about dopamine. NINDS notes that people with PD also lose cells that make norepinephrine, a messenger the body uses for automatic jobs like keeping blood pressure steady. That may explain some symptoms that aren't about movement, such as tiredness and blood pressure changes. NINDS also lists other symptoms: depression or anxiety (sometimes even before movement problems), sleep problems, speech changes, bladder problems, and digestive problems like nausea and constipation.

Who gets Parkinson's? NINDS says the average age when symptoms start is the early to mid-60s, and the chance rises with age. A small percentage of people get "early-onset" PD at younger ages. In most people the cause is unknown; researchers think both genes and environmental exposures play a part.

## 14.4 A Protein That Folds Wrong: Alpha-Synuclein and Lewy Bodies

Proteins are like long strings that must fold into exact shapes to work, the way a paper airplane only flies if it's folded correctly. Crumple it the wrong way, and it's useless. Worse, some wrongly folded proteins stick together into clumps.

**Alpha-synuclein** is a protein found in neurons, especially at their tips where they send signals, that can misfold and clump together; these clumps are a hallmark of Parkinson's disease. In healthy neurons it is thought to help with releasing chemical messengers, though its normal job is still being studied.

When alpha-synuclein misfolds, it forms clumps inside neurons. A **Lewy body** is an abnormal round clump, made mostly of misfolded alpha-synuclein, found inside nerve cells in Parkinson's disease and some related conditions. NINDS says damaged brain cells in most people with PD have Lewy bodies, though researchers don't yet know exactly why they form or how much harm they do themselves. Pathologists also see thinner threads of the same protein in nerve fibers, called Lewy neurites. Together, these are what scientists mean by "Lewy pathology."

Here's the part that made scientists think about the gut. In some diseases, a misfolded protein can act like a bad template. When it touches a normal copy of the same protein, it can push that copy to misfold too, and the misfolding spreads.

**Prion-like spread** is the idea that a misfolded protein, such as alpha-synuclein, can pass from one neuron to a connected neuron and make normal copies of the protein misfold, so the damage moves along nerve pathways over time. The name comes from prions, the misfolded proteins behind mad cow disease. "Prion-like" does **not** mean Parkinson's is contagious between people. It describes how the protein may spread from cell to cell inside one person's nervous system.

Think of falling dominoes. The first domino tips the next, which tips the next, and the wave follows the line of dominoes wherever it goes. In 2003, the German neuroanatomist [Heiko Braak and his colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12721813/) used that very image, a falling row of dominoes, to describe how Lewy pathology seems to march through connected brain regions in a set order. If the dominoes follow nerve connections, then where is the first domino? That question leads straight to the gut.

    Alpha-synuclein, Lewy bodies, prion-like spread: that's a lot at once. Here's my shortcut: alpha-synuclein is the protein, a Lewy body is the clump it forms, and prion-like spread is the idea that the clumping moves from neuron to neuron like dominoes.

## 14.5 Clues Before the Tremor: Prodromal Symptoms

Doctors use the word "prodrome" for early warning signs that come before the main symptoms of a disease. **Prodromal symptoms** are early signs that can appear years before the main symptoms of a disease and before it can be diagnosed; in Parkinson's they include constipation, loss of smell, acting out dreams during sleep, and mood changes.

[The National Institute on Aging (NIA)](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/parkinsons-disease/parkinsons-disease-causes-symptoms-and-treatments) says many people with Parkinson's notice that before their stiffness and tremor began, they had sleep problems, constipation, loss of smell, and restless legs. NINDS adds that depression or anxiety may appear early, even before movement problems. One sleep problem gets special attention: REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD), in which a person physically acts out vivid dreams, kicking, punching, or shouting, because the normal "freeze" of the muscles during dreaming sleep doesn't work.

The table below collects the main prodromal signs you just read about. Notice that none of them is about movement.

| Early sign | What the person notices | Part of the nervous system that may be involved |
|---|---|---|
| Constipation | Fewer, harder bowel movements | Gut nerves (enteric nervous system) and the vagus nerve |
| Loss of smell | Food tastes flat; can't smell smoke or flowers | Smell centers in the brain |
| REM sleep behavior disorder | Acting out dreams, sometimes injuring a bed partner | Brainstem circuits that control sleep |
| Depression or anxiety | Low mood, worry | Several brain systems, including ones that make serotonin and norepinephrine |

### Constipation: the most famous gut clue

**Constipation** means having fewer bowel movements than usual (often fewer than three a week), or stools that are hard, dry, or difficult to pass. It's very common. [The National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK)](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/constipation/definition-facts) says about 16 out of 100 adults have constipation symptoms, rising to about 33 out of 100 adults aged 60 and older. It usually has ordinary causes, like low fiber, not enough fluid, travel, stress, or certain medicines.

So how strong is the link to Parkinson's? In 2016, [Adams-Carr and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26345189/) combined 9 observational studies including 741,593 people. People with constipation had about twice the odds of being diagnosed with Parkinson's later (an odds ratio of 2.27). Even when they looked only at constipation that started **more than 10 years** before diagnosis, the link held (an odds ratio of 2.13). Doctors had already recognized constipation as a non-motor feature of Parkinson's; this study showed it can come more than a decade before diagnosis.

Now the most important part, especially if you or someone you love is constipated. An odds ratio of about 2 is a **relative** number. It compares two groups. It does not tell you that constipation is likely to lead to Parkinson's. Parkinson's is uncommon, so doubling a small chance still gives a small chance. The great majority of constipated people never develop Parkinson's. Constipation is a clue that researchers can study across huge groups of people, not a warning sign for any one person.

!!! mascot-thinking "Twice as likely, but of what?"
    ![Vee thinking](../../img/mascot/thinking.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Whenever you hear "twice as likely," ask "twice as likely as what?" Doubling a small chance still leaves a small chance, and that's exactly why constipation tells researchers a lot about Parkinson's while telling a single constipated teen almost nothing.

Notice what kind of evidence this is: observational studies of people, showing a correlation over time. Constipation coming first fits the idea that the gut is involved early. But it could also mean that the disease affects many parts of the nervous system at once, and gut nerves simply show it first. Timing alone can't prove where the disease began.

## 14.6 The Braak Hypothesis: Does the First Domino Fall in the Gut?

In 2003, Braak's team published two influential papers. In the first, they examined the brains of many people who had died with Parkinson's, and of some people who had Lewy pathology but had never shown symptoms. They proposed a staging system. Lewy pathology, they reported, first shows up in two places: a cluster of neurons low in the brainstem that sends fibers down the vagus nerve (the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus), and a smell-processing area at the front of the brain. From the brainstem, the pathology seems to climb upward through connected regions, reaching the substantia nigra in the middle stages and the outer brain (the cortex) last.

The second paper made a bolder suggestion. Some people who had no symptoms already had Lewy pathology in both the gut's nervous system and the brainstem vagus nucleus. Braak's team asked whether an unknown trigger, perhaps something swallowed, might cross the gut lining, get into enteric neurons, and travel up the vagus nerve into the brain, tipping dominoes all the way.

The **Braak hypothesis** is the proposal, published by Heiko Braak and colleagues in 2003, that Parkinson's pathology spreads through the nervous system in a predictable order, beginning low in the brainstem and in the smell system, and that in some cases it may start in the gut and travel up the vagus nerve to the brain. Notice the two parts: a **staging pattern** in the brain (based on what they saw at autopsy) and a **gut origin idea** (a hypothesis about where it starts).

Why was this exciting? It tied together several puzzles. It explained why constipation and smell loss can come years before movement symptoms, and why the vagus nucleus is hit so early. It also suggested new ways to detect Parkinson's early, perhaps by sampling gut tissue, long before the dopamine neurons are mostly gone.

Why is it still a hypothesis? Autopsy studies show where pathology is at the moment of death, not where it started. It's like finding fallen dominoes after the fact: you can guess where the first one fell, but you didn't see it fall. And not every brain fits the pattern. In 2019, [Borghammer and Van Den Berge](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31498132/) pointed out that a small fraction of people with Parkinson's don't have pathology in the vagus nucleus at all, which is hard to explain if every case climbs up from the gut.

Here's how this book grades the popular version of the idea.

!!! hype-moderate "Hype Meter: \"Parkinson's can start in the gut\""
    <div class="hype-scale"><span>Strong</span><span>Moderate</span><span>Preliminary</span><span>Hype</span></div>
    **Verdict: Moderate (as a hypothesis for some patients).** Human autopsy studies show Lewy pathology early in the vagus nucleus and sometimes in gut nerves, large observational studies show constipation can come more than 10 years before diagnosis, and mouse experiments show misfolded alpha-synuclein can travel from gut to brain along the vagus nerve. But human evidence is mostly correlation, studies of vagus surgery and appendix removal in people disagree, and some patients don't fit the pattern at all. Saying "Parkinson's starts in the gut" for everyone is too strong; "Parkinson's may start in the gut in some people" is a fair summary, and that narrower claim is what this grade covers.
    **What would change the grade:** Long-term human studies that track alpha-synuclein in the gut and brain of the same people over many years, and show the gut changes come first and predict who develops the disease, could move it up. Large studies showing no gut-first group would move it down.

You can find how each grade is defined on [the Hype Meter page](../../hype-meter.md).

## 14.7 Testing the Idea in Mice

If the dominoes really can fall from the gut to the brain, you should be able to start them in the gut on purpose and watch. You can't do that in people, but you can in mice.

In 2019, [a team at Johns Hopkins](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31255487/) led by Sangjune Kim, Ted Dawson, and Han Seok Ko did exactly that. They made small, pre-formed fibers of misfolded mouse alpha-synuclein (called "seeds") and injected them into the muscle wall of the upper gut of mice. Then they looked at the brains at 1, 3, 7, and 10 months.

The misfolded protein showed up first in the vagus nucleus of the brainstem, then in other parts of the lower brainstem, and much later in regions including the amygdala and the substantia nigra. The mice gradually lost dopamine neurons and developed movement problems as well as non-movement problems, such as poorer scores on memory and smell tests. Then came the key test. In mice whose vagus nerve was cut (a truncal vagotomy), and in mice bred without the alpha-synuclein protein, the spread to the brain didn't happen, and neither did the neuron loss.

!!! claim-box "How to read a gut-brain claim: the 2019 gut-to-brain mouse study"
    | Question | This study |
    |---|---|
    | Mouse or human? | Mouse only. No people were studied. |
    | Correlation or cause? | Cause, within the mouse model. Researchers injected seeds on purpose and showed that cutting the vagus nerve, or removing alpha-synuclein, blocked the spread. But the seeds were injected by a needle, which is not how Parkinson's begins in people. |
    | How many? | Small groups: about 7 to 12 mice per group in the behavior tests. |
    | How long? | Up to 10 months after injection, which is a large part of a mouse's life. |
    | Who paid for it? | The JPB Foundation and the US National Institutes of Health (National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke), with support from the Adrienne Helis Malvin Medical Research Foundation. The authors declared no competing interests. |
    | Is something being sold? | No. This was basic research with no product. |

The conclusion: this careful experiment shows that the gut-to-brain route is **possible** in mice, and that the vagus nerve can carry misfolded alpha-synuclein. It does not show that this is how Parkinson's starts in people, or how often. The first domino in a human, if it is in the gut, would not be a needle full of lab-made protein.

A second famous mouse study came from Caltech in 2016. [Sampson and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27912057/) used mice engineered to make too much alpha-synuclein. Mice raised germ-free (with no microbes) or treated with antibiotics had fewer movement problems, while adding gut microbes back made the problems worse. Mice that received gut microbes from people with Parkinson's developed worse movement problems than mice that received microbes from healthy donors. This suggests gut microbes can **influence** how a Parkinson's-like disease plays out in this mouse model. It does not show that microbes cause Parkinson's in people.

!!! mascot-warning "A mouse is not a mini-person"
    ![Vee warning](../../img/mascot/warning.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    When a headline says "Scientists prove Parkinson's starts in the gut," check whether the study used mice. These two studies are strong experiments, but they show what can happen in a mouse model, not what does happen in people.

## 14.8 Testing the Idea in People: Vagus Surgery and the Appendix

Scientists can't cut people's vagus nerves to test a hypothesis. But for decades, surgeons cut the vagus nerve for another reason: to reduce stomach acid in people with severe ulcers, before modern ulcer medicines made the operation rare. (Chapter 2 tells that story.) That history created a natural experiment.

**Truncal vagotomy studies** are large health-record studies that compare people who had their main vagus nerve trunks surgically cut (a truncal vagotomy, usually for ulcers) with people who did not, to test whether cutting the gut-to-brain vagus route lowers the later risk of Parkinson's disease. A "selective" or "superselective" vagotomy cuts only some branches, so it acts as a useful comparison: if the vagus route matters, a full cut should protect more than a partial one.

Here's what two big studies found.

- **Denmark, 2015 (Svensson and colleagues).** The team used [national registries](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26031848/) of people who had a vagotomy between 1977 and 1995. Overall, truncal vagotomy was not clearly linked to Parkinson's risk (a hazard ratio of 0.85, a result that could easily be chance). But among people followed for more than 20 years, those with a truncal vagotomy had about half the risk of the general population (a hazard ratio of 0.53). The researchers noted that the estimates weren't very precise, and the confidence interval for that result came very close to "no difference." Superselective vagotomy showed no change.
- **Sweden, 2017 (Liu and colleagues).** [This larger study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28446653/) compared 9,430 people who had a vagotomy (3,445 truncal) with 377,200 matched people. Overall, vagotomy was **not** linked to Parkinson's risk (a hazard ratio of 0.96). There was only a suggestion of lower risk after truncal vagotomy (0.78, which could be chance), and a lower risk when the truncal vagotomy happened at least 5 years before diagnosis (0.59).

So: some, but not all, results point the way the gut-first idea predicts, and the clearest signals come from small subgroups. These are also observational studies. People who needed vagus surgery may differ from other people in smoking, diet, other illnesses, or medicines, and those differences could affect Parkinson's risk too. The Danish team did adjust for several of these, but no adjustment is perfect.

Vagus surgery is also not a way to prevent Parkinson's. The operation has real side effects (Chapter 2's bedside case describes some), and the evidence doesn't come close to supporting it as prevention.

### The appendix puzzle

The appendix is a small, finger-shaped pouch attached to the large intestine, packed with immune tissue and nerves. **Appendix and Parkinson's** refers to the research question of whether removing the appendix (an appendectomy) changes a person's later risk of Parkinson's disease, based on the idea that the appendix might store misfolded alpha-synuclein that could seed the disease.

The results are a good lesson in why one study is never the final word.

| Study | What it found |
|---|---|
| [Killinger et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30381408/) (two data sets, more than 1.6 million people) | Appendix removal decades before diagnosis was linked to **lower** Parkinson's risk. Healthy human appendixes also contained clumped alpha-synuclein. |
| [Svensson et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27621223/) (Denmark, 265,758 people with appendectomy) | Appendix removal was linked to a **slightly higher** risk (hazard ratio 1.14). |
| [Lu et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31538320/) meta-analysis (6 studies, about 3.55 million people) | **No** overall link (relative risk 1.02). |
| [Chin et al. 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40761643/) meta-analysis (9 studies) | **No** overall link (relative risk 1.01). |

The 2018 study made headlines around the world. But when researchers pooled all the available studies, the effect disappeared. The finding that healthy appendixes contain clumped alpha-synuclein is still interesting, because it shows the gut can hold the protein in its problem form. But it doesn't mean the appendix starts the disease, and removing it doesn't seem to change risk overall.

!!! hype-hype "Hype Meter: \"Removing your appendix prevents Parkinson's\""
    <div class="hype-scale"><span>Strong</span><span>Moderate</span><span>Preliminary</span><span>Hype</span></div>
    **Verdict: Hype.** One large 2018 study linked appendix removal to lower Parkinson's risk, but a Danish study of 265,758 people found a slightly higher risk, and two meta-analyses (6 studies with about 3.55 million people, and 9 studies in 2025) found no overall link. No study tested appendix removal as prevention, and surgery has its own risks.
    **What would change the grade:** New, large studies that consistently find lower risk after appendix removal, with a clear biological explanation, could move the association to Preliminary. Even then, it would not make surgery a prevention strategy.

!!! living-with "If you're going through this"
    If you have a gut condition like IBS, Crohn's, or ulcerative colitis, reading about constipation and Parkinson's might make you worry. Please hold onto the numbers: Parkinson's mostly begins in older adults, the constipation link comes from comparing huge groups, and most people with gut symptoms never develop it. Your gut condition is its own diagnosis with its own care plan. If you're worried, write the question down and ask your care team; they can explain what your symptoms do and don't mean.

## 14.9 Two Routes? Gut-First and Brain-First Parkinson's

If some patients fit Braak's climb from the brainstem and others don't, maybe there's more than one kind of Parkinson's. In 2019, the Danish researcher Per Borghammer and his colleague Nathalie Van Den Berge proposed exactly that.

The **gut-first subtype** (also called "body-first") is a proposed form of Parkinson's disease in which alpha-synuclein pathology starts in the gut or other body nerves, reaches the brainstem through the vagus nerve, and climbs up to the dopamine neurons later. Because the body's automatic nerves and the brainstem sleep circuits are damaged early, this subtype is predicted to have constipation, other automatic-nerve problems, and REM sleep behavior disorder years **before** movement symptoms.

The **brain-first subtype** is a proposed form of Parkinson's disease in which alpha-synuclein pathology starts inside the brain, reaches the dopamine system early, and spreads down to the brainstem and the body's nerves later. This subtype is predicted to show movement symptoms earlier, with REM sleep behavior disorder and gut problems usually absent before diagnosis and appearing later, if at all.

Before we compare them, notice that both subtypes share the same machinery from Section 14.4: misfolded alpha-synuclein and prion-like spread along nerve connections. What differs is where the first domino falls. The table below lays out the predictions.

| Feature | Gut-first subtype | Brain-first subtype |
|---|---|---|
| Proposed starting place | Gut or other body nerves | Inside the brain (the exact site is debated) |
| Route to the dopamine neurons | Up the vagus nerve, through the brainstem | Directly within the brain |
| Constipation and other automatic-nerve problems | Early, often years before diagnosis | Later |
| REM sleep behavior disorder before diagnosis | Common | Usually absent |
| Movement symptoms | Come later in the sequence | Come earlier in the sequence |

Borghammer and Van Den Berge supported their proposal with brain imaging studies comparing people with and without REM sleep behavior disorder, plus autopsy findings, and noted that it could explain several puzzles in the earlier research. But they called it a hypothesis themselves, a framework for designing new studies. It is one of the most active debates in Parkinson's research.

In the brain-first route, cutting the vagus changes nothing in the brain, because the disease never needed that road. Ask yourself: if the human vagotomy studies had found a big, clear protective effect, which hypothesis would that support? What does the mixed result you read in Section 14.8 suggest?

!!! model-limits "What this model leaves out"
    - It is a model of two competing hypotheses, not a proven map. Many scientists think real Parkinson's is messier, and some think neither route explains every case.
    - The years, the regions chosen, and the order of connections are simplified teaching values; real timelines vary widely from person to person, and nobody can watch the spread happen in a living human.
    - Real spread involves both sides of the brain, many more regions, and probably genes, aging, inflammation, and the environment, which the model ignores.
    - In the model, cutting the vagus fully blocks gut-first spread, as it did in mice; human vagotomy studies give mixed results, and other nerve routes may exist.
    - Symptoms appear at a single fixed threshold here. In real life, symptoms like constipation and low mood have many other causes, and many people with them never develop Parkinson's.

## 14.10 The Gut Matters for Treatment Too: Levodopa Absorption

Even if Parkinson's turns out to start in the brain for most people, the gut still matters every single day for people living with it, because of how the main medicine works.

People can't simply take dopamine, because dopamine in the blood doesn't easily cross the blood-brain barrier, the protective layer of cells that controls what gets into the brain. Instead, NINDS explains, the main Parkinson's medicine is levodopa, which the brain converts into dopamine. It's almost always combined with a second medicine, carbidopa, which keeps the body from breaking levodopa down too early, so more of it reaches the brain.

Levodopa is usually swallowed as a pill, so it has a long trip before it can work. It has to leave the stomach, get absorbed into the blood from the upper part of the small intestine, and then cross into the brain. **Levodopa absorption** is the process by which levodopa taken by mouth passes from the gut into the bloodstream; in Parkinson's it can be delayed or uneven because of slow stomach emptying, constipation, and competition from protein in meals.

Each step can go wrong:

- **Slow stomach emptying.** Parkinson's can slow the stomach. A 2020 review by [Pfeiffer and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32461054/) says delayed stomach emptying has been reported in a substantial fraction of people with Parkinson's and is linked to delayed levodopa levels in the blood and more "off" periods, times when symptoms come back because the medicine isn't working well.
- **Constipation and other gut problems.** A 2023 review by [Leta and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36757008/) lists swallowing problems, slow stomach emptying, constipation, a stomach infection called *H. pylori*, bacterial overgrowth in the small intestine, and changes in gut microbes as possible barriers to levodopa reaching the blood.
- **Protein in meals.** Levodopa is carried across the gut wall by the same transport system that carries some amino acids (the building blocks of protein). A 2019 review by [van Kessel and El Aidy](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2019.01087/full) notes that dietary amino acids can interfere with levodopa absorption, and NINDS notes that too much protein may limit how well people absorb carbidopa-levodopa.

This is why people with Parkinson's sometimes have unpredictable days when the medicine kicks in late or not at all. Their neurology team may adjust timing, treat the gut problems, or use forms of treatment that skip the gut entirely. Those decisions belong to the care team; NINDS warns that people should never stop carbidopa-levodopa suddenly without talking with a doctor.

## 14.11 Alzheimer's Disease: Is the Gut Involved?

Now for the other big neurodegenerative disease. If Parkinson's is the disease of movement, Alzheimer's is the disease of memory and thinking.

**Alzheimer's disease** is a progressive brain disease that slowly destroys memory and thinking skills and, eventually, the ability to carry out everyday tasks; it is the most common cause of dementia in older adults. The Alzheimer's Association's [2026 Alzheimer's Disease Facts and Figures report](https://www.alz.org/alzheimers-dementia/facts-figures) estimates that about 7.4 million Americans aged 65 and older are living with Alzheimer's in 2026. Dementia means losing thinking, remembering, and reasoning abilities to the point that it interferes with daily life.

In 1906, Dr. Alois Alzheimer examined the brain of a woman who had died after memory loss, language problems, and unpredictable behavior. He found two kinds of abnormal deposits. Scientists now call them amyloid plaques and tau tangles, and the NIA says they're still considered main features of the disease.

**Amyloid beta** is a small protein fragment, cut from a larger protein in the brain, that can clump together into sticky plaques between neurons; these plaques are one of the main signs of Alzheimer's disease. Tau is a different protein that normally helps hold up the internal "railway tracks" of neurons; in Alzheimer's, it twists into tangles inside the cells. According to the NIA, these changes can begin a decade or more before symptoms appear, and damage often starts in memory areas such as the hippocampus.

Notice the pattern you saw in Parkinson's: a misfolding protein, a long silent period, and spread through connected regions. That has led researchers to ask whether gut microbes might nudge Alzheimer's too.

The human evidence is very early. In 2017, [Vogt and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29051531/) compared stool samples from 25 people with Alzheimer's dementia and 25 people without it, matched for age and sex. The Alzheimer's group had less diverse gut bacteria and a different mix of bacterial groups, and some bacterial levels correlated with Alzheimer's markers in spinal fluid. That's interesting, but it's a single snapshot of 50 people.

It can't tell us which came first. People with dementia often eat differently, move less, take different medicines, and live in different settings, and any of those could change their gut microbes. In a 2024 review, [Seo and Holtzman](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38172602/) described research on gut microbes and Alzheimer's as "still in their infancy."

That hasn't stopped supplement marketing.

!!! hype-hype "Hype Meter: \"Probiotics prevent Alzheimer's\""
    <div class="hype-scale"><span>Strong</span><span>Moderate</span><span>Preliminary</span><span>Hype</span></div>
    **Verdict: Hype.** People with Alzheimer's have different gut bacteria in small studies, such as one comparing 25 patients with 25 matched controls, and mouse studies (summarized in a 2024 review by Seo and Holtzman) suggest microbes may affect amyloid buildup in mice. But a 2024 review describes the field as being in its infancy, and no large, long human trial has shown that any probiotic prevents Alzheimer's. The difference in microbes could be a result of the disease or of lifestyle, not a cause.
    **What would change the grade:** Large, long randomized trials in people, following them for years and showing fewer Alzheimer's diagnoses or slower decline with a specific product, could move it to Preliminary or higher. You'll learn how to read probiotic trials in [Chapter 16](../16-probiotics-psychobiotics/index.md).

!!! families "For families"
    If a grandparent or parent has Parkinson's or Alzheimer's, you might be the one who notices changes first, like constipation, smell loss, low mood, or medicine that seems to wear off at certain times. These are worth mentioning to their doctor, because many of them are treatable. Try not to blame them for being slow, forgetful, or withdrawn; these are symptoms of the disease, and depression and anxiety in Parkinson's can be treated. Be cautious with supplements that promise to "protect the brain through the gut," and bring any product to the care team before trying it.

## At the Bedside: Nanaji's Long Road to a Diagnosis

!!! note "Meet the patient (fictional)"
    Nanaji is Riya's 72-year-old grandfather. Over the past two years his right hand has shaken at rest, his handwriting has gotten tiny, and he walks with small steps. Looking back, his family remembers other changes: constipation for many years, losing his sense of smell, and, according to Riya's grandmother, shouting and swinging his arms in his sleep as if fighting someone in a dream. Lately he seems quieter and more worried than he used to be.

**What the doctor checks:** A neurologist takes a careful history, including the non-movement symptoms that came first, and asks about medicines that can cause Parkinson-like symptoms. The physical exam looks for tremor at rest, rigidity, slowness, and balance problems. There's no single blood test for Parkinson's; the diagnosis is mainly based on the history and exam. In some cases, brain imaging helps rule out other causes. A sleep study can confirm REM sleep behavior disorder.

**Connecting the signals:** Nanaji has the classic movement symptoms caused by dopamine neuron loss. But his story also includes years of constipation, smell loss, and acting out dreams before the tremor. In the gut-first hypothesis, this order of events is what you'd predict if alpha-synuclein pathology began in body nerves and climbed through the brainstem's sleep circuits before reaching the substantia nigra. His doctor explains that this is an interesting fit, but no test can tell where his disease actually started.

**Diagnosis:** Parkinson's disease.

**Why it happens:** Dopamine-making neurons in his substantia nigra have been dying for years, probably along with neurons in his gut, smell system, and brainstem. Lewy bodies made of misfolded alpha-synuclein are found in damaged cells in most people with Parkinson's. Why the process started in him is unknown, as it is for most patients.

**The mind-body side:** Depression and anxiety are common in Parkinson's and can even appear before movement problems, according to NINDS. They are part of the disease, not a weakness, and they're treatable with therapy, medicine, or both. Nanaji's team screens for low mood, and his family learns that his quietness may be a symptom, not a sign that he's lost interest in them. Staying active and connected helps too; Riya plans a weekly cooking session with him and her grandmother.

**Treatment today:** The main treatment is carbidopa-levodopa, which usually works well for movement symptoms early in the disease but doesn't stop it from progressing. Because the medicine must be absorbed from the gut, his team also treats his constipation, for example with more fiber and fluids as NINDS suggests, and pays attention to meal timing and protein, which can affect levodopa absorption. Physical, occupational, and speech therapy help with walking, daily tasks, and voice. Exercise is encouraged. All medicine choices and changes are made with his neurologist.

**What's new in research:** In 2023, the Parkinson's Progression Markers Initiative reported results for a test that detects misfolded alpha-synuclein "seeds" in spinal fluid ([Siderowf and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37059509/), *Lancet Neurology*). Among 1,123 participants, the test was positive in 87.7% of people with Parkinson's and negative in 96.3% of healthy controls, and it found the seeds in 44 of 51 people with early warning signs (REM sleep behavior disorder or smell loss) who had not been diagnosed.

The test missed more often in some groups, though: it was positive in only 67.5% of people whose Parkinson's is linked to variants in a gene called *LRRK2*. Several of the study's authors work for Amprion, the company that developed the test.

In 2024, two expert groups used tests like this one to propose new "biological" definitions and stages of Parkinson's that start with finding misfolded alpha-synuclein rather than with symptoms (Simuni and colleagues; Höglinger and colleagues, both in *Lancet Neurology*). Both groups said their systems are for research only, and experts still debate them, partly because the test can be negative in some people with typical Parkinson's. **Evidence grade: Moderate** as a research tool for identifying Parkinson's biology; it is not a routine screening test for the general public, and it can't say where the disease began.

??? question "Your turn: reason through a variation"
    Nanaji's friend Mr. Okafor, also in his 70s, developed a tremor and slowness, but he never had constipation, smell loss, or dream-acting beforehand. His movement symptoms got worse fairly quickly, and only later did he develop constipation. Which proposed subtype does his story fit better, and what's the limit of that conclusion?

    **Answer:** His story fits the brain-first subtype better: movement symptoms first, without REM sleep behavior disorder or gut problems beforehand, and gut problems later. But the subtypes are a hypothesis, symptoms like constipation have many causes, and memory of past symptoms can be fuzzy. No test available in routine care can show where his pathology started, so this is a teaching exercise, not a diagnosis.

## Common Misconceptions

**Myth:** If you're constipated, you'll probably get Parkinson's.  
**Reality:** People with constipation have about twice the odds of being diagnosed with Parkinson's later, but that's a relative comparison across huge groups. Constipation is very common and has many causes, Parkinson's mostly begins in older adults, and the great majority of constipated people never develop it.

**Myth:** The Braak hypothesis has been proven.  
**Reality:** Braak's staging pattern is based on autopsies, which show where pathology is at death, not where it started. Mouse studies show gut-to-brain spread is possible, but human studies of vagus surgery are mixed, and a small fraction of patients don't fit the pattern. It's an influential hypothesis, not a settled fact.

**Myth:** Removing your appendix prevents Parkinson's.  
**Reality:** One 2018 study found a lower risk, but another large study found a slightly higher risk, and two meta-analyses found no overall link. Appendix removal is not a prevention strategy.

**Myth:** Alzheimer's is caused by gut bacteria.  
**Reality:** Small studies, such as one comparing 25 people with Alzheimer's to 25 without, found differences in gut bacteria. These are correlations, and the disease itself, along with diet, medicines, and activity, could change the microbes. The field is described by experts as still in its infancy.

**Myth:** "Prion-like" means Parkinson's is contagious.  
**Reality:** "Prion-like spread" describes how misfolded alpha-synuclein may pass from one neuron to a connected neuron inside one person's nervous system. It does not mean you can catch Parkinson's from someone.

??? question "3. Explain: Why did cutting the vagus nerve block the spread of misfolded alpha-synuclein to the brain in the 2019 mouse study?"
    The misfolded protein was injected into the gut wall, and the vagus nerve is the main nerve line linking the gut to the brainstem's vagus nucleus. Prion-like spread passes from neuron to connected neuron, so the protein needs an unbroken chain of nerve cells to travel. Cutting the vagus removed that chain, so the protein couldn't reach the brainstem, and the dopamine neuron loss and behavior problems didn't happen.

??? question "4. Apply: Nanaji's levodopa seems to kick in late on days when he's very constipated and after big protein-heavy lunches. Use Section 14.10 to explain why."
    Levodopa has to leave the stomach and be absorbed from the upper small intestine before it can reach the brain. Slow gut movement, which often goes along with constipation in Parkinson's, can delay that trip. Protein in meals breaks down into amino acids that compete with levodopa for the same transport system in the gut wall, so less levodopa gets absorbed, or it gets absorbed later. His care team, not his family, should decide on any changes to timing or diet.

**5. Read the data.** In 2017, Liu and colleagues used Swedish health registers to compare people who had a vagotomy with matched people who didn't. Here are their hazard ratios for later Parkinson's disease (1.0 means no difference; below 1.0 means lower risk). The numbers in parentheses are 95% confidence intervals: the range of values the data are compatible with.

| Group compared with matched reference people | Hazard ratio (95% CI) |
|---|---|
| Any vagotomy (9,430 people) | 0.96 (0.78 to 1.17) |
| Truncal vagotomy (3,445 people) | 0.78 (0.55 to 1.09) |
| Truncal vagotomy at least 5 years before diagnosis | 0.59 (0.37 to 0.93) |

??? question "5. What does this data show, and what can't it show?"
    Overall, vagotomy wasn't linked to Parkinson's risk: 0.96 is close to 1.0, and the interval includes 1.0. For truncal vagotomy, the estimate is lower (0.78), but the interval still includes 1.0, so it could be chance. Only the subgroup with truncal vagotomy at least 5 years before diagnosis has an interval entirely below 1.0, which is consistent with the gut-first idea. But this is an observational study, so it can't prove that cutting the vagus caused the lower risk; people who had surgery may differ in other ways, and testing many subgroups raises the chance of a lucky result. A helpful follow-up would be a pooled analysis of all vagotomy studies, using the same definitions and long follow-up.

## Key Takeaways

- Parkinson's disease is a neurodegenerative disease in which dopamine neurons in the substantia nigra die; most are gone before movement symptoms like tremor, rigidity, and slowness appear.
- Misfolded alpha-synuclein forms Lewy bodies, and it may spread from neuron to connected neuron (prion-like spread), which does not mean Parkinson's is contagious.
- Prodromal symptoms, including constipation, smell loss, REM sleep behavior disorder, and mood changes, can come years earlier. Constipation roughly doubles the odds of a later diagnosis, but most constipated people never develop Parkinson's.
- The Braak hypothesis proposes that pathology climbs from the brainstem and may begin in the gut; mouse studies show gut-to-brain spread is possible, but human vagotomy studies are mixed and appendix removal shows no overall link.
- Parkinson's may come in gut-first and brain-first subtypes that predict different symptom orders. This is an active, unresolved hypothesis.
- The gut also matters for treatment: slow stomach emptying, constipation, and protein can affect levodopa absorption.
- Alzheimer's disease involves amyloid beta plaques and tau tangles. Gut microbe differences have been found in small studies, but the research is very early, and claims that probiotics prevent Alzheimer's are hype.
