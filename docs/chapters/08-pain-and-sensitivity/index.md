# Chapter 8: Pain, Sensitivity, and the Gut

## Summary

Gut pain is real, common, and strangely hard to describe. This chapter follows a pain signal from sensors in the gut wall, through the spinal cord, and up to the brain areas that turn it into an experience. Along the way you will see why gut pain is vague and often felt in the wrong place, how the spinal cord and brain can turn pain signals up or down, and why many people with IBS feel gut stretch as painful at lower levels than other people do. You will also learn why "the tests are normal" never means "the pain is imaginary," and which approaches help when the pain system itself is turned up too high.

## 8.1 Same Gas, Different Pain

Imagine two students sitting side by side in an assembly. Both ate the same bean burrito, and both have about the same amount of gas stretching their intestines. One, Riya's lab partner, doesn't notice a thing. The other, Sam, has a cramping pain low in his belly and spends the assembly planning the fastest route to the restroom. Who is right about how much their gut "should" hurt?

The answer is both of them. Sam (a fictional student who appears throughout this book) has IBS with mixed bowel habits, and gut pain is one of his main symptoms. It flares before big tests and races. He was diagnosed at 15, after tests ruled out other causes, and his gut shows no damage. Yet the pain is as real as a stubbed toe. Meanwhile his best friend Riya, who has Crohn's disease, notices that her pain changes depending on whether her disease is active. During a flare, when her intestine is inflamed, even a normal meal can hurt. In remission, the same meal is usually fine.

Sam and Riya are living examples of this chapter's big idea: **pain is not a simple measure of what is happening in the gut.** It is something the nervous system builds, and the "volume" can be set differently in different people, or in the same person on different days.

## 8.2 A Smoke Alarm With a Volume Knob

Before we name any nerves, think about a smoke alarm. It has a **detector** that senses smoke, a **circuit board** that decides whether the signal is strong enough to sound the alarm, and a **speaker** that makes the noise.

Now imagine three ways the alarm could start going off at the smell of toast:

1. The detector becomes extra sensitive, so a little smoke looks like a lot.
2. The circuit board turns up its amplifier, so even a weak signal triggers the full alarm.
3. Someone removes the "hush" button that normally lets a person quiet false alarms.

In all three cases, the toast is the same. What changed is the alarm system. The pain system works in a similar way. Sensors in the gut are the detectors. Neurons in the spinal cord are the circuit board. The brain is both the speaker and the person with the hush button. This chapter walks through each part, and the MicroSim later lets you turn each dial yourself.

**Pain perception** is the brain's construction of the conscious experience of pain from incoming nerve signals, shaped by attention, emotion, memory, and context. Scientists use a separate word, nociception, for the nerve activity caused by a harmful or possibly harmful stimulus. [The International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP)](https://www.iasp-pain.org/resources/terminology/), the main scientific organization for pain research, [revised its official definition of pain in 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32694387/). In short, it describes pain as an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience linked to actual or possible tissue damage, or one that resembles such an experience. Its notes add two points that matter for this chapter: pain and nociception are different things, and pain cannot be inferred just from activity in sensory neurons.

So nerve signals are the raw material, but pain is what the brain makes of them. That is why two people with the same gut stretch can have different pain, and why pain can be real even when no damage shows up on a scan.

!!! mascot-thinking "Pain is a report, not a ruler"
    ![Vee thinking](../../img/mascot/thinking.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    I carry a lot of gut signals up to the brain, and I can tell you the brain doesn't just read them like a ruler. It weighs them against everything else going on, then decides how loud the alarm should be. That makes pain real and adjustable at the same time.

## 8.3 The Detectors: Nociceptors

Let's start at the detector end. A **nociceptor** is a sensory nerve ending that responds to stimuli that could damage tissue, such as strong pressure, extreme heat, harsh chemicals, or the chemicals released during inflammation. IASP describes nociceptors as high-threshold receptors, meaning they normally need a strong stimulus before they fire. ("Noci" comes from a Latin word for harm.) Nociceptors are not the same as pain. They are detectors that send warning signals; pain happens only if the brain builds an experience from those signals.

Nociceptors are found almost everywhere in the body, but they behave differently in different tissues. That gives us two broad kinds of pain.

**Somatic pain** is pain that comes from the skin, muscles, bones, and joints (the "body wall"). It is usually sharp or aching and easy to point to. If you cut your finger, you know exactly which finger.

**Visceral pain** is pain that comes from internal organs (the viscera), such as the stomach, intestines, bladder, or heart. It tends to feel dull, crampy, or deep, and it is hard to pinpoint. A 2016 review of visceral pain by [Gebhart and Bielefeldt](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27783853/) explains why: internal organs have relatively sparse sensory nerve supply compared with other tissues, and they are supplied by two sets of sensory nerves that overlap in what they do. As a result, visceral sensations are diffuse and are often felt in other places on the body. The same review notes that about one-third of people with chronic pain report that internal organs contribute to their pain.

The gut's sensory endings respond especially to stretch of the gut wall and to inflammation. The table below sums up the two kinds of pain.

| Feature | Somatic pain | Visceral pain |
|---|---|---|
| Where it comes from | Skin, muscles, bones, joints | Internal organs, such as the gut |
| How it feels | Often sharp, clear | Often dull, crampy, deep, or vague |
| Easy to locate? | Usually yes | Usually no; often felt somewhere else |
| Density of sensory nerves | Dense | Sparse |
| Example | Stubbing a toe | Gas or a cramp in the intestine |

How do gut signals reach the central nervous system? In Chapter 2 you met the vagus nerve and the spinal afferent nerves. A 2013 review by [Brookes and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23438947/) describes three routes from the gut: a vagal route, and two spinal routes (one entering the spinal cord in the middle and lower back, called thoracolumbar, and one lower down, called lumbosacral). Together these carry feelings of fullness, bloating, nausea, discomfort, urgency, and pain. The roles overlap, but researchers generally describe the spinal afferents as the main pain route from the colon, while the vagus carries mostly non-painful information, such as fullness and nausea. (See [Chapter 2](../02-vagus-highway/index.md) for the vagus side.)

Brookes and colleagues also describe "silent" gut sensory endings, which matter later in this chapter. These endings are quiet most of the time and don't respond to ordinary stretch, but inflammatory chemicals can switch them on. Keep that in mind when we get to Riya's flares.

!!! living-with "If you're going through this"
    If your belly pain is hard to describe, that's not because you're bad at explaining it. Visceral pain really is vague, since the gut has fewer, more spread-out sensors than your skin. Simple notes for your care team can help: when the pain happens, what it feels like, and what was going on that day. Doctors can use patterns like these even when the pain is hard to put into words.

## 8.4 Why Gut Pain Shows Up in the Wrong Place: Referred Pain

Here is a puzzle. When the heart is starved of oxygen during a heart attack, many people feel pain not in the chest alone but in the left shoulder and arm. The open textbook *Anatomy and Physiology 2e* (OpenStax) uses exactly this example. Why would the brain put heart pain in the arm?

**Referred pain** is pain from an internal organ that is felt at a different spot on the body surface, usually skin or muscle supplied by nerves entering the same part of the spinal cord. The leading explanation, according to OpenStax, is that sensory fibers from the organ enter the spinal cord at the same level as sensory fibers from that patch of body surface. OpenStax says the brain then misreads the signal as coming from that body surface. One common way to picture it: signals from the body surface are far more frequent, so the brain bets that the trouble is there. OpenStax also notes that the pattern is not random, but the exact mechanism has not been firmly established.

Think of an apartment building where two apartments share one doorbell wire. When the bell rings, the person at the front desk usually assumes the visitor is for the apartment that gets most of the visitors. They are wrong some of the time, and that is referred pain.

Gut pain follows the same rule. In a classic [balloon study of the colon in 1973](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4696535/) (more on it soon), people felt the pain from a balloon in the lower colon in different places: about 40% in the lower middle belly, 31% in one or both sides of the lower belly, 21% in the rectum, and the rest in the back or elsewhere. Same balloon, same spot in the colon, very different locations on the body map. That is why doctors ask detailed questions about pain location, but never rely on location alone.

!!! mascot-tip "Pointing is not proof"
    ![Vee with a tip](../../img/mascot/tip.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    When your belly hurts "right here," that tells the doctor something useful, but it doesn't pin down which organ is involved. That's normal for visceral pain, so expect questions about timing, triggers, and bowel habits too.

## 8.5 The Route Up: From the Gut to the Brain

Now we follow the signal upward. You may remember the basic parts of the nervous system from biology class. Neurons pass signals to each other across junctions called synapses, and the spinal cord carries information between the body and the brain. For a deeper refresher on pain pathways, see [From Neuron to Bedside, Chapter 6](https://aaryavvatts-lab.github.io/neuroscience-textbook/chapters/06-hearing-touch-taste-smell-pain/). Here is the short version, focused on the gut.

**Step 1: The sensory neuron's home base.** A **dorsal root ganglion** is a cluster of sensory neuron cell bodies sitting just outside the spinal cord, on the nerve root that enters the back (dorsal) side of the cord. Each sensory neuron has one long branch reaching out to the gut wall and another reaching into the spinal cord. OpenStax notes that visceral sensory neurons have their cell bodies either in a dorsal root ganglion or in a sensory ganglion of a cranial nerve (for the vagus, that's the nodose ganglion from Chapter 2).

**Step 2: The first relay station.** The **dorsal horn** is the back part of the spinal cord's gray matter, where incoming sensory neurons form synapses with spinal neurons that carry the signal on toward the brain. This is the "circuit board" in our smoke alarm. As you'll see, signals can be boosted or dampened here.

**Step 3: The brain's sorting center.** The **thalamus** is a pair of egg-shaped structures deep in the middle of the brain that relays most incoming sensory information to the outer brain (the cortex). OpenStax describes it as a required transfer point for most sensory pathways on their way to the cortex. Pain signals pass through it too.

**Step 4: Where the experience is built.** From the thalamus, signals reach several cortex areas. Two matter most for gut pain:

- The **insula** is a region of cortex folded deep inside each side of the brain that maps signals from inside the body, such as gut stretch, heartbeat, and breathing. It is a key area for interoception, the sense of the body's internal state that you met in Chapter 2.
- The **anterior cingulate cortex** (ACC) is a strip of cortex near the front of the brain, along the midline just above the band of fibers that connects the two halves of the brain, that helps give pain its unpleasant, emotional, "I need to do something about this" quality and helps focus attention on it.

How do we know these areas are involved? Researchers have used brain scanners (functional MRI and similar tools) while gently stretching the rectum with a balloon. In 2011, [Tillisch, Mayer, and Labus](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20696168/) pooled the published scanning studies in a meta-analysis (a study that statistically combines the results of many earlier studies). In both people with IBS and healthy volunteers, the stretch consistently activated the thalamus, the insula, and part of the cingulate cortex called the anterior midcingulate. People with IBS showed more consistent activation in regions linked to emotional arousal, including the pregenual part of the anterior cingulate cortex and the amygdala, and in a midbrain area involved in the body's own pain control. Healthy volunteers more often activated parts of the prefrontal cortex linked to thinking and control.

This was a human study, but it shows patterns of brain activity, not cause. It can't tell us whether the brain differences cause IBS symptoms, come from years of having symptoms, or both.

    ![Vee encouraging](../../img/mascot/encouraging.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Lots of new names here, so shrink it to four stops: gut sensor, root ganglion, dorsal horn, then thalamus to the insula and ACC. Every "volume knob" in the rest of this chapter sits at one of those stops.

## 8.6 The Gate in the Spinal Cord

Have you ever bumped your elbow and rubbed it without thinking? Rubbing seems to make it hurt less. In 1965, [Ronald Melzack and Patrick Wall](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5320816/) published a famous paper in the journal *Science*, called "Pain mechanisms: a new theory," that offered an explanation.

**Gate control theory** is the idea, proposed by Melzack and Wall in 1965, that the dorsal horn of the spinal cord works like a gate that can let pain signals through to the brain or partly block them, depending on other incoming signals and on signals coming down from the brain. As a [2022 review by Clifford Woolf](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpain.2022.984042/full) summarizes it, signals from large touch fibers activate small inhibitory neurons in the dorsal horn, which partly close the gate on signals from thin pain fibers. Activity in the thin pain fibers does the opposite and opens the gate. The theory also proposed that the brain itself could send signals down to adjust the gate.

So rubbing the elbow sends a flood of touch signals that partly close the gate on the pain signals from the same spot. That's the elbow trick.

Gate control theory changed how scientists think about pain: it is not a straight wire from injury to brain. But Woolf's 2022 review argues that the original wiring diagram is now outdated and oversimplified, since real spinal circuits are far more complex. A fair summary: the circuit Melzack and Wall drew was too simple, but their big idea, that pain signals are adjusted on the way up, has held up.

## 8.7 The Brain's Volume Knob: Descending Pain Modulation

The gate is not controlled only from below. The brain can reach down and adjust it. **Descending pain modulation** is the brain's ability to turn incoming pain signals down (or up) by sending signals from the brainstem down to the dorsal horn of the spinal cord. A key area for this is the periaqueductal gray, a ring of gray matter around a fluid channel in the midbrain, which works with other brainstem areas.

In the smoke alarm comparison, this is the person with the hush button. Stress can push this system in either direction. A sudden burst of intense stress or fear can turn pain down for a while, which helps explain why an athlete may not notice an injury until after the game; researchers call this stress-induced analgesia. Ongoing stress, worry, and close attention to pain tend to work the other way. In animal studies, many kinds of stress make the gut more sensitive to stretch, and in people, stress is linked to IBS symptoms starting or getting worse. Calm, distraction, and a sense of control can push the system toward turning pain down.

One way researchers test descending control is with a trick: give someone a strong, painful stimulus in one part of the body and see whether pain somewhere else gets weaker. In a healthy nervous system, it often does, as if the brain dampens pain signals across the body when a strong new pain arrives. Researchers call this the "pain inhibits pain" effect, or diffuse noxious inhibitory controls (in human studies it is now usually called conditioned pain modulation). The study below used this test, and a Hype Meter grade follows it (see [the Hype Meter](../../hype-meter.md) if you need a reminder of what the grades mean).

In 2004, [Wilder-Smith and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15479679/) in Switzerland scanned the brains of 10 women with IBS (5 with constipation and 5 with diarrhea) and 10 healthy women. A balloon gently stretched the rectum to cause pain, and the women rated the pain from 0 (none) to 10 (worst). Then the researchers repeated the stretch while each woman put her foot in painfully cold ice water. In healthy women, the ice water lowered rectal pain scores (by a median of 1.5 points on the 10-point scale). In the women with IBS, the ice water did not significantly lower rectal pain. Brain activity in areas including the periaqueductal gray, cingulate cortex, insula, and amygdala also differed between the groups.

!!! claim-box "How to read a gut-brain claim: the 2004 ice-water study"
    | Question | This study |
    |---|---|
    | Mouse or human? | Human. Adult women only. |
    | Correlation or cause? | It compares groups, so it shows a difference linked to IBS, not what caused it. The ice water was an experiment within each person, but nobody was assigned to have IBS. Weaker pain control could contribute to IBS pain, or years of pain could change pain control, or both. |
    | How many? | Very few: 10 women with IBS and 10 healthy women. Results this small can change when repeated. |
    | How long? | One scanning session per person. It can't show whether the difference lasts or changes with treatment. |
    | Who paid for it? | The paper says the study was partly supported by a research grant from AstraZeneca, a drug company. |
    | Is something being sold? | No product is tested or sold in the paper. |

The careful conclusion: this small study supports the idea that the brain's built-in pain brakes may work differently in some people with IBS. It does not prove that weak pain brakes cause IBS, and it doesn't tell us about men, teens, or people with other gut conditions. That's why the idea gets a Preliminary grade below.

!!! hype-preliminary "Hype Meter: "In IBS, the brain's pain brakes don't work""
    <div class="hype-scale"><span>Strong</span><span>Moderate</span><span>Preliminary</span><span>Hype</span></div>
    **Verdict: Preliminary.** In a 2004 human study of 20 women, a painful ice-water stimulus to the foot reduced rectal pain in healthy women but not in women with IBS, and a meta-analysis of brain scanning studies found differences in a region involved in pain control. ## 8.8 When the Alarm Is Set Too Sensitive: Visceral Hypersensitivity

Now we can name the main idea that connects all of this to IBS. **Visceral hypersensitivity** is a lowered threshold for feeling sensations from internal organs, so that normal amounts of gut stretch or activity are felt as uncomfortable or painful. In smoke alarm terms, the alarm goes off at toast.

How do scientists measure something as personal as gut sensitivity? With a balloon. The **balloon distension test** is a research and specialty test in which a thin, soft balloon is placed in the rectum or colon and slowly or quickly inflated with set volumes or pressures while the person reports when they first feel it, when it feels uncomfortable, and when it hurts. Some versions use a device called a barostat that controls the pressure precisely.

The key study is more than 50 years old. In 1973, the physician J. Ritchie published a study in the journal *Gut*. He inflated a balloon in the lower (pelvic) colon of 67 patients with what was then called "irritable colon syndrome" (today's IBS) and 16 comparison volunteers. When the balloon was inflated to 60 milliliters, 55% of the patients felt pain, compared with only 6% of the comparison group. Ritchie also estimated the tension in the gut wall at that volume and found it was normal in the patients. Their gut wall wasn't being stretched harder. Their nervous systems were reporting the same stretch as pain.

Later studies built on this. In 1995, [Mertz and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7797041/) at UCLA tested 100 people with IBS and 15 healthy controls and reported that 94% of the IBS group showed some form of altered rectal sensation: feeling discomfort at lower volumes, feeling sensations more strongly, or feeling them in unusual places. The hypersensitivity showed up only with fast inflation, not slow. That 94% came from one center and counted any kind of altered sensation, so later reviews describe hypersensitivity as common but not universal. A 2013 review by [Farmer and Aziz](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26516496/) calls it a key, but not universal, feature. The best summary: **many, but not all, people with IBS have visceral hypersensitivity.**

Does sensitivity actually matter for symptoms? In 2018, [Simrén and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28104632/) combined data from five groups of patients in different countries, 1,144 people with IBS or functional dyspepsia (a disorder of upper-gut pain and fullness), all of whom had had balloon testing. In every one of the five groups, people with higher sensitivity reported more severe gut symptoms. This link remained after the researchers accounted for anxiety, depression, and a general tendency to report body symptoms. The effects were small to medium in size, and the study shows an association, not proof of cause, but it's consistent across countries and methods.

Most people with IBS never have a balloon test. Doctors diagnose IBS from symptoms and a few targeted tests, as you'll see in [Chapter 10](../10-ibs-and-dgbi/index.md). Balloon tests are mainly tools for understanding the condition, not for diagnosing a single patient.

!!! hype-strong "Hype Meter: "People with IBS feel gut stretch as painful at lower levels, on average""
    <div class="hype-scale"><span>Strong</span><span>Moderate</span><span>Preliminary</span><span>Hype</span></div>
    **Verdict: Strong.** Human balloon studies since 1973 have repeatedly found that, as a group, people with IBS report pain at lower stretch than people without it (55% vs 6% at one volume in the first study), and a 2018 analysis of 1,144 patients in five cohorts linked higher sensitivity to worse symptoms. The words "on average" matter: many people with IBS have normal sensitivity on testing.
    **What would change the grade:** Little would lower the group finding. Studies showing exactly why some people become hypersensitive, and whether treating it improves symptoms for that subgroup, would add the missing "why."

## 8.9 Turning Up the Detector: Peripheral Sensitization

Visceral hypersensitivity can come from changes at different stops along the pathway. Let's start at the gut end.

**Peripheral sensitization** is an increase in how strongly sensory nerve endings in a tissue respond, along with a drop in the stimulus needed to make them fire, usually caused by inflammation or injury in that tissue. IASP defines it as increased responsiveness and a reduced threshold of nociceptive neurons in the periphery. In smoke alarm terms, the detector itself is now extra twitchy.

Inflammation is the classic trigger. When tissue is inflamed, immune cells release chemicals (you met cytokines in [Chapter 4](../04-immune-sense-organ/index.md)) that act directly on nerve endings and make them easier to fire. Remember the "silent" endings from Section 8.3 that wake up in response to inflammatory chemicals? They add to the signal too.

This is what happens to Riya during a Crohn's flare. Her inflamed intestine sends stronger signals from the same meal, so ordinary digestion hurts. When treatment calms the inflammation and she returns to remission, the detectors usually settle back down. The pain followed the inflammation.

What about IBS, where there's no visible inflammation? Some research points to small-scale immune activity. In 2004, [Barbara and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14988823/) in Italy studied colon biopsies (tiny tissue samples) from 44 people with IBS and compared them with healthy controls. Thirty-four of the 44 (77%) had more of the lining taken up by mast cells, the immune cells you met in [Chapter 7](../07-stress-and-the-gut/index.md) that release chemicals such as histamine. Only the mast cells close to nerve fibers (within 5 micrometers, far less than the width of a hair) were linked to how often and how badly people felt pain. This is human evidence, but it's a correlation in one group of patients, not proof that mast cells cause IBS pain.

One more finding matters for people with IBD. A 2020 meta-analysis by [Fairbrass and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33010814/) pooled studies of 3,169 adults with IBD whose disease was in remission. About 32.5% reported symptoms that matched IBS criteria. When remission was confirmed by looking inside the bowel with a scope, the figure was lower but still about 23.5%. People with these IBS-type symptoms also had higher anxiety and depression scores. In other words, some people with IBD keep having gut pain even after the inflammation has calmed, which suggests the pain system itself may have stayed turned up. This is an association in adults, so it can't tell us the cause, and it doesn't apply automatically to teens.

## 8.10 Turning Up the Amplifier: Central Sensitization

Now move from the detector to the circuit board. **Central sensitization** is an increase in the excitability of pain-processing neurons in the spinal cord and brain, so that normal or weak incoming signals produce stronger pain responses. In smoke alarm terms, the detector works normally, but the circuit board has cranked up its amplifier.

In 2011, [the pain researcher Clifford Woolf](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20961685/) described central sensitization in a review as a prolonged but **reversible** increase in the excitability and strength of connections in central pain pathways. It can be triggered in healthy volunteers by strong painful stimuli to the skin, muscles, or internal organs, and it can be seen in changes in brain activity. Woolf lists visceral pain hypersensitivity disorders among the conditions in which central sensitization appears to contribute, along with fibromyalgia, headaches, and some joint and nerve pain.

Two words in that description deserve attention.

- **Reversible** means the amplifier can be turned back down. Central sensitization is a change in how neurons are behaving, not permanent damage.
- **Appears to contribute** means scientists think it plays a role, but it is not the only cause, and there is still no simple test that measures it in one person.

The two kinds connect. Strong or long-lasting signals from an inflamed gut can themselves trigger central sensitization, which may help explain why some people keep having pain after a gut infection or during IBD remission. This idea is still being studied.

!!! mascot-warning "Central does not mean imaginary"
    ![Vee warning](../../img/mascot/warning.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    "Central sensitization" means real neurons in the spinal cord and brain are firing more strongly, which you could measure in a lab. It never means "made up." When the amplifier is turned up, the pain is exactly as real as pain from an injury.

Now drop the stretch to 3: the signal still travels up the spinal cord, but it stays below the threshold, so it is sensed but not felt as pain. That's like most of the gut activity you never notice. Set the stretch back to 6 and raise peripheral sensitivity to 2x. The same stretch now hurts much more, which is roughly what happens to Riya during a flare.

Next, reset and raise only the central gain. Notice that a boost in the spinal cord gives the same felt pain as the same boost in the gut. The meter can't tell where the amplification happened, and neither can a pain score in a clinic. Finally, with central gain at 2x, slide the brain state from "very calm" to "very stressed," the kind of swing Sam feels before a race. The gut hasn't changed at all, yet the pain moves several points.

!!! model-limits "What this model leaves out"
    - Real pain is built from many signals at once (stretch, chemicals, inflammation, motility), not one stretch slider.
    - Peripheral and central sensitization interact over time; strong gut signals can cause central sensitization, which the sim treats as separate, fixed dials.
    - The brain's influence includes attention, memory, expectations, mood, and sleep, not just a single calm-to-stressed slider. In the sim, "stressed" always turns pain up, but a sudden, intense stress can sometimes turn pain down for a while (stress-induced analgesia).
    - The vagus nerve, the many brain regions involved, and the emotional side of pain are left out or shown as single dots.
    - The threshold, the multipliers, and the 0 to 10 output are made-up teaching numbers. The sim cannot predict any real person's pain.

## 8.12 Thoughts, Feelings, and the Volume of Pain

The brain doesn't adjust pain only through automatic circuits. What we expect, fear, and pay attention to also feeds into the descending system. This is where many people with chronic pain start to feel blamed, so let's be careful and precise.

**Pain catastrophizing** is a pattern of thinking about pain in which a person tends to dwell on it, expect the worst from it, and feel helpless to do anything about it. The term comes from pain psychology. The most widely used questionnaire, the Pain Catastrophizing Scale (developed by Sullivan and colleagues in 1995), measures three parts: rumination (not being able to stop thinking about the pain), magnification (seeing the pain as more threatening), and helplessness (feeling nothing can be done).

The name sounds harsh, but it doesn't mean "overreacting." It describes a common, understandable pattern that often grows when pain is unpredictable, unexplained, or not believed. Researchers study it because it seems to turn the pain volume up, possibly through descending pathways, and because it can change with practice.

For example, in a 2018 study of 261 children with IBS (average age about 10), [Hollier and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6651721/) found that pain catastrophizing partly accounted for the link between anxiety and how severe the children's abdominal pain was. Children with more anxiety tended to catastrophize more, and more catastrophizing went with worse pain. The authors pointed out that their design (measuring everything at one time point) can't prove cause. It's a human, correlational finding, but it suggests that thinking patterns about pain may be a useful target for treatment.

This connects to treatment. Brain-gut behavioral therapies, such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and gut-directed hypnotherapy, teach skills to change how the brain responds to gut signals. In a 2024 network meta-analysis of 42 randomized trials with 5,220 people with IBS, [Goodoory and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38777133/) found that several of these therapies, including self-guided CBT and face-to-face gut-directed hypnotherapy, may improve abdominal pain more than control treatments. No trial was at low risk of bias in every area, so the size of the benefit may be overestimated.

The [2021 American College of Gastroenterology IBS guideline](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33315591/) also suggests gut-directed psychotherapy for overall IBS symptoms ("suggests" is the guideline's word for a conditional recommendation, weaker than "recommends"). Some doctors also use certain medicines at low doses as "gut-brain neuromodulators," which act on nerve signaling to dial pain down; using them doesn't mean a person's pain is caused by depression or anxiety. You'll learn more about these treatments in [Chapter 10](../10-ibs-and-dgbi/index.md) and [Chapter 18](../18-sleep-exercise-therapy/index.md).

None of this says "the pain is psychological." Researchers think these therapies work partly through the same pain-control circuits that the brain scans point to. Changing the brain's response to a real signal is a real treatment for a real problem.

Living with pain that comes back again and again can also wear down a person's mood. Long-lasting pain, poor sleep, and worry tend to feed each other, and people with chronic gut pain have higher rates of anxiety and depression symptoms, as [Chapter 11](../11-anxiety-depression-gut/index.md) explains. If pain is making you feel hopeless, that deserves care just as much as the pain itself.

!!! living-with "If you are struggling right now"
    If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, call or text **988** to reach the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (in the US), or go to the nearest emergency room. Outside the US, you can find a helpline at [findahelpline.com](https://findahelpline.com/). You can also talk to a parent, school counselor, or doctor.

!!! families "For families"
    When a teen's tests come back normal, it can be tempting to feel relieved and say, "See, there's nothing wrong." To a teen in pain, that can sound like "we don't believe you." Try "I'm glad it's nothing dangerous. Let's figure out how to make it hurt less." Avoid asking "Is it really that bad?" and instead ask what helps and what makes it worse. Helping a teen keep up with school, sleep, and friends, while working with the care team on a plan, usually helps more than either pushing through or stopping everything.

## 8.13 "It's All in Your Head": Why That Phrase Is Wrong

Let's put the chapter together and grade the claim that hurts many people with gut pain the most.

Normal blood work, stool tests, or a colonoscopy show that certain diseases (like inflammation, bleeding, infection, or cancer) are unlikely. They don't measure how sensitive the nerves are, how much the dorsal horn amplifies signals, or how well the brain's pain brakes work. Those are real biological processes, but they don't show up on a standard scan.

A 2019 review by [Grundy, Erickson, and Brierley](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30379615/) estimates that chronic visceral pain affects more than 20% of people worldwide (including pelvic and bladder pain, not only the gut), often with no obvious structural cause. If "normal tests" meant "imaginary," a huge share of humanity would be imagining their pain.

!!! hype-hype "Hype Meter: "If your tests are normal, the pain is in your head""
    <div class="hype-scale"><span>Strong</span><span>Moderate</span><span>Preliminary</span><span>Hype</span></div>
    **Verdict: Hype.** Standard tests look for structural disease, not for how sensitive the pain system is. Human balloon studies since 1973 show that many people with IBS feel normal gut stretch as painful while their gut wall tension is normal, and brain scans show real differences in how the brain processes gut signals. Pain always involves the brain, but that makes it real, not imaginary.
    **What would change the grade:** Nothing realistic. The careful version, "normal tests can mean the pain comes from a sensitized pain system rather than damage," is already well supported.

## At the Bedside: A Year of Belly Pain and Normal Tests

!!! note "Meet the patient (fictional)"
    Nadia is 17 and a high school senior. For about a year she has had cramping lower-belly pain several days a week, often after meals and a little better after a bowel movement. Her stools switch between loose and hard. She has quit her dance class and skips lunch because she worries the pain will hit in class. An emergency room visit ended with "Everything looks fine," and her mom has started to wonder if Nadia is exaggerating. So has Nadia.

**What the doctor checks:** A pediatric gastroenterologist takes a detailed history: when the pain happens, how it relates to bowel movements, what her stools are like, and whether there are warning signs such as blood in the stool, weight loss, waking at night with diarrhea, fever, or a family history of IBD or celiac disease. Nadia has none. The doctor examines her belly and orders a few targeted tests: blood tests, a celiac blood test, and a stool test for inflammation (fecal calprotectin, which you'll meet in Chapter 9). All are normal. No balloon test is needed, because IBS can be diagnosed from symptoms plus limited testing.

**Connecting the signals:** Normal tests make inflammation, celiac disease, and other structural problems unlikely. But her months of pain linked to bowel movements and stool changes fit a disorder of gut-brain interaction. The doctor uses a picture like this chapter's smoke alarm: her nervous system is likely turning normal gut activity up into pain (visceral hypersensitivity), and her fear of pain in class may be raising the volume further through descending pathways.

**Diagnosis:** Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) with mixed bowel habits, a disorder of gut-brain interaction. (Chapter 10 covers how IBS is diagnosed.)

**Why it happens:** Visceral hypersensitivity can come from peripheral sensitization in the gut, central sensitization in the spinal cord and brain, and differences in descending pain modulation. Researchers don't yet know which mix applies to Nadia. Her pain is real because the signals and the amplification are real.

**The mind-body side:** The doctor says clearly, "Your pain is real, even though your tests are normal," and Nadia cries with relief. The team suggests her mom believe Nadia's reports while helping her slowly return to routines like dance. Nadia is referred to a psychologist who works with gut conditions, and her school counselor sets up a restroom pass so she doesn't have to explain herself.

**Treatment today:** Her plan may include learning how the gut-brain pain system works, a brain-gut behavioral therapy such as CBT or gut-directed hypnotherapy, a visit with a dietitian, and possibly a medicine her doctor chooses, such as a low-dose gut-brain neuromodulator. Her doctor explains that these medicines act on pain signaling and don't mean her pain is "really depression." Medicine decisions belong to Nadia, her family, and her doctor.

**What's new in research:** In 2026, [Algera and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41247041/) in Sweden reported a non-invasive test of gut sensitivity in which people drink a sugar solution (lactulose with a nutrient drink) and rate symptoms such as pain and bloating for 4 hours. The researchers set the cutoff for "hypersensitive" from how 133 healthy volunteers responded, and by that cutoff, 76% of 273 people with IBS were hypersensitive. Those with lower rectal pain thresholds on balloon testing were more likely to be hypersensitive, but so were people whose gut bacteria made more hydrogen gas from the sugar, so the test measures gas production as well as nerve sensitivity. **Evidence grade: Preliminary.** It is one study from a single research group that defined its own cutoff, and other centers need to repeat it before it could become a routine test.

??? question "Your turn: reason through a variation"
    Riya has been in remission from Crohn's disease for eight months. Her latest stool calprotectin and her colonoscopy showed no active inflammation, but she has started having crampy belly pain again before orchestra concerts. Using this chapter, how would you reason about this?

    **Answer:** The first step belongs to her care team: make sure there is no new inflammation, since a flare must be found and treated. If her disease really is quiet, her pain could still be real. Studies of adults with IBD in remission find that about a quarter to a third report IBS-type symptoms, possibly because earlier inflammation left the pain system more sensitive (peripheral and central sensitization), and pre-concert stress could be turning the volume up through descending pathways. ## Common Misconceptions

**Myth:** The amount of pain always matches the amount of damage in the body.  
**Reality:** Pain is built by the nervous system from signals that can be turned up or down at several stops. A gut with no visible damage can hurt a lot, and some damage causes little pain.

**Myth:** Gut pain is easy to locate, so where it hurts tells you which organ is the problem.  
**Reality:** Visceral pain is vague because internal organs have sparse, overlapping sensory nerves, and it is often referred to other spots on the body. In Ritchie's 1973 balloon study, the same balloon in the colon was felt in the lower belly, the sides, the rectum, or the back.

**Myth:** More pain always means the disease is getting worse.  
**Reality:** Pain can rise because the pain system is more sensitive, because of stress, or because of poor sleep, without any change in disease. For people with IBD, new pain still needs to be checked by the care team, but some people in remission have IBS-type pain without active inflammation.

**Myth:** Central sensitization means the pain is imaginary or "all in your head."  
**Reality:** Central sensitization is a measurable increase in the excitability of real neurons in the spinal cord and brain. It is reversible, and the pain it causes is as real as pain from an injury.

## Your Gut Check

Test yourself. Try each one before you open the answer.

??? question "1. Claim Check: "Doctors couldn't find anything wrong, so my IBS pain is just stress. If I relax enough it will go away for good.""
    **Grade: Hype.** The claim mixes something true with two leaps. It's true that stress can turn pain up through descending pathways, and in randomized trials, therapies that calm the brain's response to gut signals may reduce pain (moderate-quality evidence). But IBS pain also involves visceral hypersensitivity, which can come from peripheral and central sensitization, not stress alone, and "go away for good" promises more than any study shows. A careful version: "Normal tests don't mean my pain isn't real. Stress can make it worse, and brain-gut therapies can help many people feel less pain."

??? question "2. Predict: Sam takes a balloon distension test on a calm, rested morning, and again the morning of the state cross-country meet. Predict how his pain ratings might differ, and explain the pathway."
    His pain ratings would likely be higher on race morning, at the same balloon volume. Stress and worry can push descending pain modulation toward turning signals up at the dorsal horn, and attention to his gut may raise the volume further. His gut stretch is the same both days; what changes is how much the signal is amplified on its way to the brain. (This is a prediction from the model in this chapter, not a result from a specific study of race days. A sudden, intense stress can sometimes dampen pain instead, so the real answer could vary.)

??? question "3. Explain: Why might Riya's belly hurt after an ordinary meal during a flare, but not in remission?"
    During a flare, her inflamed intestine releases inflammatory chemicals that act on nerve endings. This peripheral sensitization lowers the threshold for those endings to fire and can wake up normally silent endings, so the same meal produces a stronger signal. When treatment calms the inflammation, the endings usually settle back toward normal sensitivity, and ordinary meals stop hurting.

??? question "4. Apply: A heart attack can cause pain in the left arm. A student says, "That proves the pain signal travels to the arm." Use referred pain to correct the student."
    The signal doesn't travel to the arm. Sensory fibers from the heart enter the spinal cord at the same level as fibers from the shoulder and arm, so they share relay neurons. The brain receives the signal on that shared line and, since arm signals are much more common, interprets it as coming from the arm. The pain is felt in the arm, but its source is the heart.

**5. Read the data.** In a classic balloon study of the colon (Ritchie, 1973, *Gut*), a balloon was inflated to 60 milliliters in the lower colon of each person. Here is how many felt pain:

| Group | Number of people | Felt pain at 60 mL |
|---|---|---|
| People with irritable colon syndrome (IBS) | 67 | 55% |
| Comparison group (healthy and constipated volunteers) | 16 | 6% |

??? question "5. What does this data show, and what can't it show?"
    It shows that, as a group, people with IBS were far more likely to feel pain at the same balloon volume: 55% versus 6%. Since Ritchie also found normal gut wall tension in the patients, it supports a more sensitive pain system, not a harder stretch. It can't show cause (did sensitivity lead to IBS, or did IBS lead to sensitivity?), and the comparison group was tiny (16 people, so 6% is about 1 person) and included constipated volunteers. It also can't tell us about any individual, since 45% of the patients felt no pain at that volume. A helpful follow-up: a larger study that measures sensitivity before people develop IBS, such as after a gut infection, and follows them over time.

## Exercises

### Remember

1. What is the difference between a nociceptor and pain?

??? note "Answer"
    A nociceptor is a sensory nerve ending that responds to stimuli that could damage tissue. Pain is the experience the brain builds from signals like these, shaped by context, attention, and emotion. Nociceptor activity can happen without pain, and pain cannot be inferred just from sensory neuron activity.

2. List the four main stops a gut pain signal passes on its way to being felt.

??? note "Answer"
    Sensory nerve endings in the gut, the dorsal root ganglion (where the sensory neuron's cell body sits), the dorsal horn of the spinal cord, and the thalamus, which relays signals to cortex areas such as the insula and anterior cingulate cortex.

### Understand

3. Explain why visceral pain is harder to locate than somatic pain.

??? note "Answer"
    Internal organs have relatively sparse sensory nerves, supplied by two overlapping sets of nerves, so the signals are diffuse. Visceral fibers also share spinal relay neurons with body-surface fibers, so the brain often refers the pain to skin or muscle. The skin, by contrast, has dense sensory nerves and a precise body map.

4. In your own words, explain the difference between peripheral sensitization and central sensitization.

??? note "Answer"
    Peripheral sensitization happens at the detector end: nerve endings in the gut become more responsive, often because of inflammation. Central sensitization happens in the spinal cord and brain: pain-processing neurons become more excitable, so even normal incoming signals cause stronger pain. Both can lead to visceral hypersensitivity, and strong peripheral signals can trigger central sensitization.

## Key Takeaways

- Pain perception is built by the brain from nerve signals. Pain and nociception are different, and pain can't be read directly from sensory neuron activity.
- Visceral pain from the gut is dull, vague, and often referred to other places on the body, because the gut has sparse, overlapping sensory nerves that share spinal relay neurons with the body surface.
- A gut pain signal travels from nociceptors in the gut wall to the dorsal root ganglion, the dorsal horn, the thalamus, and cortex areas including the insula and anterior cingulate cortex.
- Pain signals are adjusted on the way up: gate control theory proposed that the spinal cord can act like a gate, and descending pain modulation lets the brain turn signals down or up.
- Visceral hypersensitivity is common, but not universal, in IBS. Balloon distension tests since 1973 show that many people with IBS feel normal gut stretch as painful, and higher sensitivity goes with worse symptoms.
- Sensitivity can be turned up at the gut (peripheral sensitization, often from inflammation) or in the spinal cord and brain (central sensitization, which is real and reversible), and thinking patterns such as pain catastrophizing can raise the volume further.
- Normal tests never mean imaginary pain. In randomized trials, brain-gut therapies such as CBT and gut-directed hypnotherapy can reduce IBS pain (moderate-quality evidence), likely by working on real pain circuits.
