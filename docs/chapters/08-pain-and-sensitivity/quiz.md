#### Question 1

What is a nociceptor?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A brain area that creates the emotional feeling of pain
2. A sensory nerve ending that responds to stimuli that could damage tissue
3. A spinal cord neuron that blocks all pain signals
4. A chemical released by mast cells during inflammation
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. A nociceptor is a high-threshold sensory nerve ending that responds to harmful or possibly harmful stimuli, such as strong pressure or inflammatory chemicals. Option A describes areas like the anterior cingulate cortex; nociceptors only detect, and pain itself is built by the brain.

    **Concept Tested:** Nociceptor
    **Bloom's Level:** Remember

#### Question 2

Which structure acts as a relay station that passes most incoming sensory signals, including pain signals, on to the cortex?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The dorsal root ganglion
2. The insula
3. The enteric nervous system
4. The thalamus
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The thalamus, deep in the middle of the brain, is a required transfer point for most sensory pathways on their way to the cortex. The dorsal root ganglion (A) holds the cell bodies of sensory neurons outside the spinal cord, so it comes earlier in the pathway.

    **Concept Tested:** Thalamus
    **Bloom's Level:** Remember

#### Question 3

What did Melzack and Wall's gate control theory (1965) propose?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The dorsal horn of the spinal cord can let pain signals through or partly block them, depending on other signals
2. Pain signals travel in a straight, unchangeable line from the injury to the brain
3. The gut has a gate that closes when food is digested
4. Pain is felt only when the brain releases a pain chemical
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Gate control theory proposed that the dorsal horn works like a gate, with touch signals helping close it and pain-fiber signals opening it, and with input from the brain adjusting it. Option B is the older "straight wire" view the theory challenged. Today the original circuit is considered too simple, but the idea that signals are adjusted on the way up has held.

    **Concept Tested:** Gate Control Theory
    **Bloom's Level:** Remember

#### Question 4

Why is pain from the intestines usually harder to pinpoint than pain from a cut finger?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The intestines have no sensory nerves at all
2. Gut pain signals travel only through the vagus nerve, which can't carry location
3. Internal organs have sparse, overlapping sensory nerves, and their signals share spinal relays with the body surface
4. Gut pain is weaker, so the brain ignores where it comes from
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The viscera have relatively sparse sensory nerve supply from two overlapping sets of nerves, so the signals are diffuse, and they share spinal relay neurons with skin and muscle, which leads to referred pain. Option A is wrong: the gut has sensory nerves, which is why stretch and inflammation can hurt.

    **Concept Tested:** Visceral Pain
    **Bloom's Level:** Understand

#### Question 5

In Ritchie's 1973 balloon study, 55% of patients with irritable colon syndrome felt pain at 60 mL versus 6% of the comparison group, yet the gut wall tension in the patients was normal. What does the normal wall tension suggest?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The patients' guts were being stretched much harder than the comparison group's
2. The patients' nervous systems were reporting the same stretch as more painful
3. The patients were pretending to feel pain
4. The balloon was broken during the patients' tests
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. If the gut wall was under normal tension, the stretch itself wasn't stronger. The difference was in how the nervous system handled the signal, which is visceral hypersensitivity. Option C confuses "no extra stretch" with "no real pain"; the pain was real, but the sensitivity was higher.

    **Concept Tested:** Balloon Distension Test
    **Bloom's Level:** Understand

#### Question 6

Which statement best describes central sensitization?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A reversible increase in the excitability of pain-processing neurons in the spinal cord and brain
2. Permanent damage to the brain caused by long-lasting pain
3. Pain that a person imagines because they are worried
4. Increased sensitivity of nerve endings in an inflamed gut
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Central sensitization is a measurable, reversible increase in how strongly central pain neurons respond, so weak or normal signals cause stronger pain. Option D describes peripheral sensitization, which happens at the nerve endings in the tissue, not in the spinal cord and brain. Option C is a harmful myth: the pain is real.

    **Concept Tested:** Central Sensitization
    **Bloom's Level:** Understand

#### Question 7

During a Crohn's flare, Riya notices that an ordinary dinner makes her belly hurt, even though the same meal is fine when she's in remission. Which mechanism best explains the difference?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Gate control: the touch fibers in her belly have stopped working
2. Referred pain: the pain is coming from her arm
3. Pain catastrophizing: she is exaggerating the pain
4. Peripheral sensitization: inflammation makes the gut's nerve endings fire more easily
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Inflammatory chemicals released during a flare act on nerve endings in the gut, lowering their threshold and waking up normally silent endings, so the same meal produces a stronger signal. Option C wrongly treats a biological change as exaggeration; nothing in her story suggests her thinking changed, but her gut's inflammation did.

    **Concept Tested:** Peripheral Sensitization
    **Bloom's Level:** Apply

#### Question 8

A soccer player twists her ankle badly in the final minutes of a big game but doesn't notice much pain until she sits on the bench afterward. Which idea from this chapter best explains this?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Visceral hypersensitivity
2. Peripheral sensitization
3. Descending pain modulation
4. Referred pain
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. During intense focus and excitement, signals from the brainstem down to the dorsal horn can turn incoming pain signals down. Once she relaxes, that dampening fades and the pain is felt. Option B would make the ankle hurt more, not less, and it develops as the tissue becomes inflamed.

    **Concept Tested:** Descending Pain Modulation
    **Bloom's Level:** Apply

#### Question 9

In a 2004 study of 10 women with IBS and 10 healthy women, painful ice water on the foot lowered rectal pain in the healthy women but not significantly in the women with IBS. Which conclusion does this study best support?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The brain's built-in pain-dampening system may work differently in some people with IBS
2. Weak pain brakes are proven to cause IBS in everyone
3. Ice water is an effective treatment for IBS pain
4. Men with IBS have stronger pain brakes than women with IBS
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The study suggests a difference in descending pain control in this small group, which is why it earns a Preliminary grade. Option B overreaches: 20 people, one session, and a group comparison can't prove cause or apply to everyone. Option D can't be judged, since no men were studied.

    **Concept Tested:** Descending Pain Modulation
    **Bloom's Level:** Analyze

#### Question 10

A 2020 meta-analysis found that about 32.5% of adults with IBD in remission reported IBS-type symptoms, but only about 23.5% when remission was confirmed by looking inside the bowel with a scope. What is the best explanation for the difference?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Scopes cause IBS-type symptoms
2. Some people counted as "in remission" by symptom scores may still have had hidden inflammation causing pain
3. IBS-type symptoms are imaginary in people with IBD
4. The scope studies included only children
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Symptom-based definitions of remission can miss ongoing inflammation, which would add inflammation-driven pain to the "IBS-type" group. Even with strict scope-confirmed remission, about a quarter still had symptoms, which suggests a sensitized pain system in some people. Option D is wrong because the meta-analysis studied adults.

    **Concept Tested:** Visceral Hypersensitivity
    **Bloom's Level:** Analyze
