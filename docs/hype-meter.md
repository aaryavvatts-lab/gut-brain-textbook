# The Hype Meter

The gut-brain axis is real science, and it's also one of the most oversold topics in wellness. A finding in 12 mice can become "this supplement cures anxiety" by the time it reaches your feed. So every chapter of this book grades popular claims with the **Hype Meter**.

!!! mascot-thinking "Why grade claims at all?"
    ![Vee thinking](img/mascot/thinking.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    A claim can be built on real science and still go way past what the science shows. The Hype Meter keeps the real part and flags the stretch.

## The four grades

!!! hype-strong "Strong"
    <div class="hype-scale"><span>Strong</span><span>Moderate</span><span>Preliminary</span><span>Hype</span></div>
    Consistent evidence in **people**, usually from large or repeated studies, randomized trials, or well-established physiology. Example: the gut has its own network of neurons that can run reflexes without the brain.

!!! hype-moderate "Moderate"
    <div class="hype-scale"><span>Strong</span><span>Moderate</span><span>Preliminary</span><span>Hype</span></div>
    Good evidence in animals plus some human evidence, **or** a strong, repeated link in people whose cause is still unclear. Example: eating lots of ultra-processed food is linked to more depression symptoms in large observational studies, but whether the food causes the symptoms is still unclear.

!!! hype-preliminary "Preliminary"
    <div class="hype-scale"><span>Strong</span><span>Moderate</span><span>Preliminary</span><span>Hype</span></div>
    Small, early, short, or mostly animal studies. Interesting, worth more research, and not ready to guide decisions. Example: probiotics for depression.

!!! hype-hype "Hype"
    <div class="hype-scale"><span>Strong</span><span>Moderate</span><span>Preliminary</span><span>Hype</span></div>
    A popular claim the evidence does not support, or one that stretches a real finding far beyond what it shows. Example: "70% of your immune system lives in your gut."

## How to read a gut-brain claim: six questions

Every chapter applies these six questions to one real study.

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Mouse or human?** | Many gut-brain findings come from mice, including germ-free mice raised with no microbes at all. Mouse results often don't carry over to people. |
| **Correlation or cause?** | Two things happening together doesn't mean one causes the other. The arrow might point the other way, or a third factor might explain both. |
| **How many?** | Small studies swing wildly by chance. A result in 18 people is a hint, not an answer. |
| **How long?** | A two-week change might not last, and a long-term harm might not show up yet. |
| **Who paid for it?** | Funding doesn't make a study wrong, but it's worth knowing who benefits from the result. |
| **Is something being sold?** | If the claim ends with a product, a test kit, or a program, read it twice as carefully. |

## What the Hype Meter is not

- It is **not medical advice**. A "Strong" grade for a treatment doesn't mean it's right for you. Only your care team can decide that.
- It is **not permanent**. Science changes. When new evidence moves a grade, the change is logged on the [Accuracy and Corrections](accuracy.md) page.
- It grades **the claim as stated**. "The gut has hundreds of millions of neurons" is Strong. "Your gut is a second brain that thinks and feels" is Hype, even though it starts from the same fact.
