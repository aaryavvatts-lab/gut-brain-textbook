## Chapter 1: The Brain in Your Belly (and Why That Phrase Is Misleading)   (slug: 01-brain-in-your-belly, sim: ch01-peristalsis-builder)
- **Hook:** Riya's first day back at school after her Crohn's diagnosis: her gut "has a mind of its own." Does it? Open with the fact that the gut keeps moving food along even when the nerve connections to the brain are cut (classic isolated-gut experiments, Bayliss and Starling 1899; Trendelenburg 1917).
- **Running example use:** Riya in the hook; Sam's "butterflies" before a race as a teaser for later chapters.
- **Bedside case:** A fictional newborn with Hirschsprung disease (no enteric neurons in the last part of the colon): delayed first stool, swollen belly; rectal biopsy shows missing ganglion cells; surgery removes the segment. Shows what happens when the ENS is missing. Mind-body side: parents' worry and how care teams support families.
- **Hype Meter cards:** "Your gut is a second brain that thinks and feels" (Hype as stated; the structure fact is Strong). Introduce the Hype Meter and the six claim questions here in full.
- **MicroSim:** Peristalsis Builder: a section of gut tube with a food bolus; the reader toggles "ENS on/off," adjusts stretch sensitivity, and watches the peristaltic reflex (contraction behind, relaxation ahead) push the bolus. With the ENS off, the bolus stalls (as in Hirschsprung). Objective: explain the peristaltic reflex as a local ENS circuit. Bloom: Understand.
- **Misconceptions:** the ENS "thinks"; the gut needs the brain to move food; "second brain" means equal to the brain; more neurons in the gut than the brain (false: the brain has about 86 billion).
- **Watch out for:** ENS neuron count is an estimate (use the canonical phrasing); "spinal cord" comparison should be phrased as "about as many as or more than the spinal cord" only if the dossier supports it.

## Chapter 2: The Vagus Highway   (slug: 02-vagus-highway, sim: ch02-vagus-signal-router)
- **Hook:** Sam feels sick to his stomach before a race and full after a few bites of lunch on test day. How does the brain know what the gut is doing?
- **Running example use:** Sam (hook, satiety); Riya's doctor explains why she feels nauseated during flares.
- **Bedside case:** A fictional adult who had a vagotomy decades ago for ulcers (historical surgery) now has early fullness and diarrhea; explains vagal efferent control of the stomach. Mind-body side: changes in eating and anxiety about meals.
- **Hype Meter cards:** "Cold plunges, humming, or gargling 'reset' your vagus nerve and cure anxiety" (Hype or Preliminary depending on the specific claim); "Most vagus fibers carry signals up to the brain" (Strong).
- **MicroSim:** Vagus Signal Router: a stylized body showing gut, vagus nerve, brainstem (NTS and dorsal motor nucleus). The reader triggers events (meal stretch, fat arriving, inflammation, a stressful thought) and sees afferent pulses travel up or efferent pulses travel down; a ratio meter shows roughly 4 of 5 fibers are afferent. Objective: compare afferent and efferent vagal signaling. Bloom: Understand/Analyze.
- **Misconceptions:** the vagus nerve is only "calming"; the vagus mostly sends commands down; vagal tone can be measured directly with a smartwatch; the vagus is the only gut-brain route (spinal afferents too).
- **Watch out for:** the afferent share (use canonical phrasing); neuropod cells were described in mice (Kaelberer 2018); don't overstate HRV as a direct vagus measure.

## Chapter 4: The Immune System as a Sense Organ   (slug: 04-immune-sense-organ, sim: ch04-cytokine-feedback-loop)
- **Hook:** When you have the flu, you want to lie in bed and avoid people. That's not weakness; it's your immune system talking to your brain (sickness behavior).
- **Running example use:** Riya's flare makes her exhausted and low; her doctor explains why inflammation affects how she feels.
- **Bedside case:** A fictional teen during a flu-like illness vs Riya's flare: both show sickness behavior; explain cytokines reaching the brain. Mind-body side: fatigue is real, not laziness.
- **Hype Meter cards:** "70% of your immune system lives in your gut" (Hype: the gut holds about 70% of antibody-making plasma cells but only about 3% of all immune cells; see CLAUDE.md canonical values); "Inflammation causes depression" (Moderate association, mechanism and cause still debated).
- **MicroSim:** Cytokine Feedback Loop: sliders for gut inflammation, sleep hours, and stress; nodes for cytokines, brain (microglia), mood, sleep, and stress with arrows that brighten as the loop strengthens; shows how a loop can reinforce itself and how breaking one link reduces the loop. Objective: explain immune-to-brain signaling as a feedback loop. Bloom: Understand/Analyze.
- **Misconceptions:** the brain is completely sealed off from the immune system; inflammation is always bad; fatigue from illness is "all in your head"; boosting the immune system is always good.
- **Watch out for:** the "70%" figure; mouse vs human for microglia; don't imply all depression is inflammatory.

## Chapter 5: Chemical Messengers and the Serotonin Myth   (slug: 05-serotonin-myth, sim: ch05-serotonin-where)
- **Hook:** A viral post: "90% of your serotonin is in your gut, so heal your gut and you'll be happy." Half true, half myth.
- **Running example use:** Sam's cousin's post; Sam's doctor explains why an SSRI can cause stomach side effects.
- **Bedside case:** A fictional teen starting an SSRI for depression who gets nausea and loose stools in the first weeks: explains gut serotonin receptors. Mind-body side: side effects are common early and are worth discussing with the doctor, not a reason to stop on one's own.
- **Hype Meter cards:** "90% of serotonin is in your gut, so your gut controls your happiness" (the number is Strong, the conclusion is Hype); "Gut bacteria make neurotransmitters that reach your brain" (Preliminary).
- **MicroSim:** Serotonin: Where Does It Go?: a body map with gut, blood (platelets), and brain separated by a blood-brain barrier; the reader releases serotonin from enterochromaffin cells, sees it act on gut nerves and get taken up by platelets, and tries to push it across the BBB (blocked); tryptophan can cross. - **Misconceptions:** gut serotonin crosses into the brain; serotonin is just a "happy chemical"; low serotonin is proven to cause depression; microbes "make your mood."
- **Watch out for:** 90 vs 95% (use canonical); serotonin's role in depression is debated (Moncrieff 2022 umbrella review controversy); be careful and neutral.
