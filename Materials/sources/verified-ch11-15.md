# Verified Source Dossier: Chapters 11-15

*Gut Check: The Neuroscience of How Your Gut and Brain Talk*
Compiled 2026-10-23. Every source below was opened (PubMed/NCBI E-utilities abstract record, Europe PMC / PMC full text, or the agency page itself) and the figures were checked against that text. Figures marked "full text" were confirmed in the open-access article body, not just the abstract.

**Hype Meter key:** Strong = consistent human evidence. Moderate = good animal plus some human, or strong association. Preliminary = small, early, or mostly animal. Hype = popular claim not supported.

**Design shorthand:** MA = meta-analysis; SR = systematic review; RCT = randomized controlled trial; OR/HR/RR = odds/hazard/risk ratio; CI = 95% confidence interval.

### (c) Watch out for

- **Symptoms are not diagnoses.** Barberio and Zamani report symptoms on screening questionnaires. Write "screen positive for anxiety symptoms," not "have an anxiety disorder." Barberio covers adults only, so do not apply 32.1%/25.2% to teens with IBD.
- **Barberio's "higher in active disease" numbers come from a smaller subset of studies.** Give them as a contrast (about 58% vs 38% for anxiety), not as a headline figure.
- **Rodent transfer studies are not human proof.** Kelly and Zheng show that depression-linked microbes *can* change animal behavior. They do not show that gut microbes cause depression in people. Grade: Preliminary.
- **Direction is unknown in human microbiome studies.** Valles-Colomer is cross-sectional. Depression changes diet, sleep, and medication use, and any of these can change gut bacteria.
- **Inflammation is an average shift, not a test.** Osimo found a right-shift across the whole group. No blood test diagnoses depression.
- **YRBS wording:** the survey item is feeling "so sad or hopeless almost every day for 2 or more weeks in a row" that the student stopped usual activities. Do not shorten it to "39.7% of teens are depressed."
- **988 details change.** The current Get Help page lists only the Spanish (press 2) and Veterans (press 1) options. It does not list an LGBTQI+ youth "press 3" option, so do not mention one. (The date that option ended was not verified here.) Recheck 988lifeline.org before publication.
- **Hype check:** claims that a probiotic or "gut reset" treats depression are not supported by any source in this dossier. Point students to proven treatments (talk therapy, medication) and to 988.
- **Safety framing:** every page that discusses suicide data should give 988 (call/text/chat) and say "call 911 if someone is in immediate danger."

### (c) Watch out for

- **"About 1%" needs a qualifier.** Biopsy-confirmed is 0.7%; blood-test positive is 1.4%. Use "about 1 in 100 people worldwide" and give both numbers in a sidebar. North America's biopsy figure is 0.5%.
- **HLA naming:** NIDDK says "DQ2 and DQ8" without the HLA prefix. Writing "HLA-DQ2/HLA-DQ8" is standard, but the 30%/3% numbers come from NIDDK. StatPearls could not be opened (bot check), so it is not cited.
- **NIDDK celiac pages were last reviewed in October 2020.** The facts here still match the 2023 ACG guideline, but recheck before print.
- **"Only treatment" wording:** say "the only proven treatment is a strict gluten-free diet." NIDDK also lists supportive care (vitamins, dapsone for the skin rash), and drugs are in trials (Lebwohl 2018).
- **Neuro numbers vary a lot.** Mearns' "up to 39%" for neuropathy is the top of a wide range across small studies. Use "up to," never "39% of patients." Gluten ataxia is uncommon (0-6%). Hadjivassiliou 2010 is a "Personal View" from one specialist group, so use it for history and concepts, not numbers.
- **Depression link is not specific to gluten.** Smith & Gerdes found depression in celiac disease is similar to depression in other chronic illnesses. The 2026 HR of 1.66 rests on only 2 celiac studies.
- **NCGS is debated, not disproven.** Blinded trials point to fructans/FODMAPs (and possibly other wheat components) rather than gluten for many people. Describe it as an open question. The symptoms are real; the trigger is uncertain. Teens should not go gluten-free before celiac testing, because blood tests need gluten in the diet to be accurate. *(General clinical point; this exact statement was not confirmed in the opened sources, so add an ACG full-text citation or cut it.)*
- **Allergy vs intolerance vs autoimmune:** NIDDK supports the lactose intolerance vs milk allergy contrast. The NIAID food-allergy page returned 403 and a JACI food-allergy review could not be opened, so **no IgE-specific source is verified here.** Either add one before writing IgE details, or keep the text at "a food allergy is an immune-system reaction" (NIDDK).
- **Hype:** "gluten harms everyone's brain" (the Grain Brain-style claim). The best available evidence (Wang 2021, n = 13,494) found no link in people without celiac disease. Grade: Hype.

### (c) Watch out for

- **Kang 2017 is n = 18, open-label, with no placebo.** Families and evaluators knew who was treated, and the protocol bundled antibiotics, a bowel cleanse, and an acid suppressant with the transplant. Always pair the result with "small," "no placebo group," and "a larger blinded trial is needed." Do not describe it as a cure.
- **Kang 2019 "47% lower" is a CARS severity rating, not a cure rate.** Do not write that "47% of children no longer had autism."
- **Yap vs Morton:** present both. Yap (n = 247) says diet explains most differences. Morton (pooled data sets) finds patterns that disappear in sibling comparisons, which also points to shared environment and diet. Neither study shows that microbes cause autism.
- **Hsiao is a mouse model.** MIA mice show "autism-like" behaviors, which is not autism. Do not say probiotics treat autistic people.
- **ADHD:** one 2026 meta-analysis (Ghosh & Singh, *Eur Child Adolesc Psychiatry*, PMID 40828192) was opened but **not used**. Its abstract reports phylum percentages that contradict its own text (it says Actinobacteria were "lower" while quoting 5.78% vs 4.89%). Use Dias 2025 instead.
- **Language:** use "autistic people" or "autistic children" (identity-first) by default. Avoid "suffers from," "cure," "at risk for autism," "special needs," and "burden." Give gut symptoms as health problems worth treating, not as evidence that autism is a disease to fix.
- **No CDC autism prevalence figure was verified for this chapter.** If writers want one, open the current CDC ADDM page first.

### (c) Watch out for

- **Constipation is common, and most constipated people never get Parkinson's.** An OR of 2.27 is a relative risk. Say this clearly so teen readers are not frightened.
- **Braak is a hypothesis, not a fact.** Borghammer notes that a small fraction of patients lack pathology in the vagal nucleus. Present "gut-first" as one possible subtype.
- **The vagotomy evidence is mixed.** Svensson's significant result (HR 0.53) is only the more-than-20-years subgroup, with a CI that nearly reaches 1. Liu found no overall effect (HR 0.96). Write "some, but not all, studies," and never imply that vagus surgery is a prevention strategy.
- **Kim 2019 and Sampson 2016 are mouse studies.** The fibrils were injected; that is not how disease starts in people. Grade: Preliminary.
- **Appendectomy:** Killinger's widely reported protective finding was not confirmed by two meta-analyses (RR 1.01-1.02) and one Danish cohort found a slight *increase*. Present it as an unresolved puzzle.
- **Alzheimer's:** Vogt is 25 vs 25 people, cross-sectional. Do not suggest that diet or probiotics prevent Alzheimer's through the gut.
- **Agency page gap:** the NINDS Parkinson's page returned HTTP 403 and was not verified. If writers need a basic PD definition (dopamine neuron loss, tremor, etc.), open NINDS or another agency page before citing.
