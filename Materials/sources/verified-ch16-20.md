# Verified Source Dossier: Chapters 16-20

*Gut Check: The Neuroscience of How Your Gut and Brain Talk*
Compiled 2026-10-23. Every entry below was opened during verification: the PubMed abstract (pulled from NCBI's PubMed record), the full text on PMC, or the agency page or PDF itself. Figures are copied from the opened source. Where a popular figure could not be confirmed, the entry says so and gives safer wording.

**Hype Meter grades:** Strong = consistent human evidence. Moderate = good animal work plus some human data, or a strong association. Preliminary = small, early, or mostly animal. Hype = popular claim the evidence does not support.

### (c) Watch out for

- **The 2024 Gut Pathogens paper does NOT show a clear depression benefit.** It is often summarized as "probiotics reduce depression (12 trials, 707 people)," but the benefit appeared only on the self-report BDI. The clinician-rated HAMD, DASS, and MADRS were not significant. Although the title says "depression and anxiety," the abstract reports only depression scales. Suggested wording: "A 2024 review of 12 trials found mixed results: probiotics helped on one depression questionnaire but not on three others."
- Meta-analyses disagree on size. Effects run from small (d about −0.24, Liu 2019; SMD −0.26, Pan 2025) to large (SMD −0.96 in clinical samples, Asad 2025), and heterogeneity is high. Don't quote one number as "the" effect.
- Don't call JB-1 a proven human treatment. The mouse result (Bravo 2011) failed in healthy men (Kelly 2017). This pair works well as the "mouse vs. human" teaching example for Chapter 20.
- Don't generalize from one strain to "probiotics." Every claim should name the strain or say "some strains."
- The AGA 2020 guideline and the FDA 2023 warning point in different directions on preterm infants. AGA conditionally suggested certain strains for NEC prevention; FDA later warned about fatal infections and said no probiotic is approved as a drug for infants. Present both, with dates.
- Don't write "the FDA approves probiotics." FDA does not approve dietary supplements before they are sold.
- The NCCIH page shows "Last Updated: August 2019" but mentions the 2023 FDA warning. For the warning itself, cite the FDA press release.

### (c) Watch out for

- **Hackam 2006 exact percentages:** The popular breakdown of the 76 animal studies (37% replicated, 18% contradicted, 45% untested) turned up only in search snippets. The JAMA letter is paywalled and has no PubMed abstract, so I could not open it. What I did open (Hackam's own 2007 BMJ article) says "just over a third." **Write "only about one in three" and cite both papers.** Don't print 37/18/45 unless someone opens the JAMA full text.
- Seok 2013 is about inflammation (burns, trauma, endotoxemia) models. Don't stretch it to "mouse studies never work."
- Ioannidis 2005 is a modeling essay, not a measurement. Don't write "most research is false." Write "a famous 2005 paper argued that small, flexible, biased studies are often wrong."
- Walter 2020's 95% (36/38) figure is about human-to-rodent microbiota transfer studies specifically.
- The FTC guidance is advice on how the FTC interprets the law. It is not a new regulation. It applies to advertising (FTC); labels fall under FDA.
- Murad 2016 doesn't say reviews are worthless. It says they are only as good as the studies inside them.
- For "how to read an abstract," NCCIH covers article parts, but the abstract-specific subpage was not opened. Teach the general structure (background, methods, results, conclusion) from the opened overview and from the real abstracts in this dossier.
