# Verified Source Dossier: Chapters 1-5

**Book:** *Gut Check: The Neuroscience of How Your Gut and Brain Talk* (grades 9-12)
**Compiled:** 2026-10-23
**Method:** I pulled every source below from its PubMed record (NCBI E-utilities efetch). Where a PMC full text was available, I opened it and searched it for the exact figure. Agency pages (NIDDK, NIH Common Fund) I opened directly. The "Exact figure/wording support" column records what the opened page actually says: short quotes (under 15 words) or close paraphrase. If a claim could only be checked against an abstract, the table says so.

**Access limits during verification (be transparent with chapter writers):**
- NCBI Bookshelf pages (StatPearls) sat behind a browser check (reCAPTCHA), which I did not try to get past. I verified StatPearls chapters through their PubMed records (abstract text only). Treat those citations as verified for the abstract wording only.
- Some full texts were paywalled: Furness 2012, Berthoud & Neuhuber 2000, Mowat & Agace 2014, Gershon & Tack 2007, Dantzer 2008 body text. For these, only the abstract was checked. Cite them only for what the table says.

**Hype Meter grades:** Strong = consistent human evidence. Moderate = good animal evidence plus some human, or a strong association. Preliminary = small, early or mostly animal. Hype = popular claim that the evidence does not support.

### (c) Watch out for

- **ENS neuron count is not one number.** Good sources give anything from ">100 million" (Rao & Gershon 2016) to "400-600 million" (Fleming 2020). Never print "exactly 500 million." Use the canonical wording at the end of this file.
- **Error in Fleming et al. 2020.** One sentence attributes Hirschsprung disease to "defective migration of enteric glia." The correct mechanism is failed migration of neural crest-derived precursors (Nagy & Goldstein 2017, Heuckeroth 2018). Do not cite Fleming for Hirschsprung mechanism.
- **Do not compare the ENS to the brain's neuron count without a source.** The verified comparison is to the spinal cord, not the brain.
- **"Second brain"** is a fine nickname, but avoid saying the gut "thinks," "decides" or "has feelings."
- **NIDDK does not mention a sex ratio.** If writers want "boys are affected more often," they need a separate verified source. I did not verify a ratio.
- **The serotonin-peristalsis story has changed.** Older textbooks say mucosal serotonin triggers peristalsis. Spencer & Hu (2020) say it is not required. Gershon (2013) notes that mouse knockout data point to neuronal serotonin mattering more for transit.
- **Sharkey & Mawe 2023:** only the abstract was checked. Cite it as a general overview only.

### (c) Watch out for

- **The "80% afferent" figure is real but old and approximate.** Its oldest traceable origin in the sources I opened is Foley & DuBois (1937), cat vagus, as cited by Prescott & Liberles (2022). That 1937 paper is not indexed in PubMed, and I did not open it. Agostoni et al. (1957, cat; PubMed 13398974) is also commonly cited, but only its bibliographic record was available, so I did not verify its numbers. Breit et al. (2018) give "80-90%" and cite a textbook, not a fiber count. Prechtl & Powley (1990) counted rat abdominal vagus fibers and concluded efferents may be over 25%. **Phrase it as:** "Roughly four out of five vagus fibers are sensory, according to classic fiber counts in animals."
- **Do not say "80-90%"** (Breit). It is the least well-sourced version.
- **Neuropod cells: say "in mice."** All the synapse and sugar-preference findings come from mouse studies.
- **Spinal afferents matter too.** Do not imply the vagus is the only gut-to-brain route. Gut pain travels mostly via spinal pathways (Brookes 2013 describes three afferent pathways). Writers should check the specific pain-route claim before printing it.
- **Ganglion naming.** Nodose = inferior vagal ganglion. Jugular = superior. In mice they are fused into one "superganglion" (Prescott & Liberles), but in humans they are separate.
- **GLP-1 drugs:** fine to mention as a real-world application (Gribble & Reimann 2019). This dossier does not verify claims about specific brand-name drugs or how much weight people lose on them.

### (c) Watch out for

- **The NIH Common Fund HMP page still says microbes outnumber human cells "by about ten to one"** (checked 2026-10-23). Do not cite that page for the ratio. Cite Sender et al. 2016. The page is fine for HMP history (2007-2016, two phases).
- **"1.3:1" is for a reference 70 kg adult male.** Sender et al. note a 25% uncertainty and 53% variation across people. Say "about the same number" or "roughly 1.3 to 1," never "exactly."
- **The microbiome ≠ only bacteria.** It also includes archaea, viruses and single-celled eukaryotes (Quince 2017). The 1.3:1 ratio counts bacteria only.
- **C-section framing.** The early-life differences are real (Shao 2019), but they shrink with age (Stewart 2018, Bäckhed 2015). Do not claim C-section birth causes lifelong disease. That has not been shown here.
- **Dominguez-Bello 2010 had only 10 babies.** Present it as the first, small study.
- **Breastfeeding lowers early diversity, and that is normal.** Do not equate "more diversity" with "healthier" for infants (Ho 2018).
- **Germ-free results are mouse results.** Grade any brain or behavior claim from germ-free mice as Preliminary unless human data exist.
- **Phylum names changed.** Bacteroidetes is now often called Bacteroidota, and Firmicutes is now often called Bacillota. Pick one naming system and say so.

### (c) Watch out for

### (c) Watch out for

- **"The gut makes 90-95% of your serotonin, so your gut controls your mood" is Hype.** The 90-95% figure is solid. The mood conclusion does not follow, because gut serotonin does not get into the brain. The brain makes its own using TPH2.
- **The blood-brain-barrier citation is still open.** I could not open a page this session that directly states "serotonin does not cross the BBB" (NCBI Bookshelf/StatPearls was blocked by a reCAPTCHA check, and the Oldendorf 1971 record has no abstract). Before printing, a writer should confirm from one of these: StatPearls "Physiology, Serotonin" (NBK545168, via a browser), or the full text of Gershon 2013 (PMC3708472) or Mawe & Hoffman 2013 (PMC4048923). The fact itself is standard physiology. Only the citation is missing.
- **"Gut bacteria make serotonin/GABA/dopamine" needs two caveats.** (1) Production is shown mainly in cultures and animals. (2) Most such molecules act locally in the gut or on nerves. Whether microbial GABA or dopamine reaches the human brain has not been shown in the sources here.
- **Yano 2015 is a mouse study.** Do not say "your gut bacteria control your serotonin." Say "in mice, certain spore-forming gut bacteria boosted gut serotonin production."
- **Do not mix up the two serotonins.** Gut serotonin (TPH1, in EC cells) and brain serotonin (TPH2, in neurons) are separate supplies.
- **Moncrieff 2023 is debated.** It was followed by published responses (corrections are linked from its PubMed record). Use it to show that "low serotonin" is too simple, not to claim antidepressants "don't work." That claim is outside what this review tested and is not supported here.
- **Do not overstate correlation as cause.** Valles-Colomer 2019 is one of the best human studies, and it is still associational.

## Canonical values

Use exactly these phrasings across all chapters.

1. **ENS neuron count:** "The enteric nervous system contains hundreds of millions of neurons, about 400 to 600 million by common estimates, which is roughly as many as the spinal cord." Sources: Fleming et al. 2020 (400-600 million; similar to spinal cord, PMID 32963521); Schneider et al. 2019 (>500 million, PMID 30379617); Rao & Gershon 2016 (>100 million; at least as many as spinal cord, PMID 27435372).

2. **Vagus afferent share:** "Most vagus nerve fibers carry signals from the body to the brain. Classic fiber counts in animals put the figure at about 80%." Sources: Prescott & Liberles 2022 (~80% sensory, citing Foley & DuBois 1937, PMID 35051375); Bonaz et al. 2018 (80/20, PMID 29467611). For the uncertainty, see Prechtl & Powley 1990 (rat efferents may be over 25%, PMID 2327594). Avoid "80-90%."

3. **Gut serotonin share:** "More than 90% of the body's serotonin is made in the gut, mostly by enterochromaffin cells. Some estimates say about 95%. This serotonin does not reach the brain, which makes its own." Sources: Yano et al. 2015 (>90%, PMID 25860609); Bellono et al. 2017 (>90% by EC cells, PMID 28648659); Gershon 2013 (95%, PMID 23222853). The BBB citation is still pending (see the Chapter 5 Watch out for list).

4. **Bacteria-to-human cell ratio:** "A typical adult has about as many bacterial cells as human cells, roughly 38 trillion bacteria to 30 trillion human cells, or about 1.3 to 1. The old '10 to 1' figure is wrong." Source: Sender, Fuchs & Milo 2016, *PLoS Biol* (PMID 27541692) and *Cell* (PMID 26824647).

5. **Gut immune-cell share:** "The gut holds about 70% of the body's antibody-making plasma cells, but only about 3% of all immune cells (about 5% of lymphocytes). The popular claim that '70% of your immune system is in your gut' is a myth." Source: Sender et al. 2023, *PNAS* (PMID 37871201), supported by Ganusov & De Boer 2007 (PMID 17964854). Origin of the myth, for example: Vighi et al. 2008 (PMID 18721321).
