### (c) Watch out for

- **Mouse vs human.** Erny 2015 (microglia), Braniste 2014 (BBB), Smith 2013 and Furusawa 2013 (Tregs), and Donohoe 2011 (colonocyte fuel) are all mouse studies. Write "in mice" in the same sentence. No human study shows that gut bacteria change BBB permeability.
- **Germ-free mice are an extreme model.** They have no microbes at all. Say they show what happens when microbes are completely missing, not what happens with an "unhealthy" microbiome.
- **The "60:20:20" acetate:propionate:butyrate ratio** is widely quoted. It was **not verified** here: the Cummings 1987 abstract says only that acetate was the main SCFA. If the book wants a ratio, check the Cummings full text (PMC1433442) or Koh 2016 full text first. Otherwise say "acetate is the most abundant."
- **"Leaky gut syndrome"** is not a recognized diagnosis. Increased permeability is real and measurable (Camilleri 2019), but supplements and diets sold to "heal a leaky gut" are unproven. Grade those claims as Hype.
- **Zonulin tests.** Do not present serum zonulin as a validated test. Two independent groups showed that common commercial kits measure other proteins (Scheffler 2018; Ajamian 2019).
- **SCFAs and mood in humans:** one 66-person trial (Dalile 2020) measured cortisol, not mood, and mood did not change. Do not write "fiber reduces anxiety."
- **Erny 2015 wording:** the abstract says SCFAs "regulated microglia homeostasis" and the FFAR2-knockout mice copied the germ-free defects. Do not overstate it as "fiber builds your brain's immune system."

### (c) Watch out for

- **Mice make corticosterone, people make cortisol.** Sudo 2004 measured corticosterone and ACTH in mice. Do not write that "germ-free mice had higher cortisol."
- **Sudo 2004 specifics:** the exaggerated response appeared with restraint stress, not with ether. *B. infantis* reversed it. Feces from normal mice helped only when given early, not later. Do not turn this into "probiotics fix stress in people."
- **CRH vs CRF:** these are the same hormone (corticotropin-releasing hormone, or factor). Pick one term and define the other once.
- **Vanuytsel 2014** is small (23 and 13 volunteers), and the effect showed up only in people whose cortisol rose. It measured small-intestine permeability with a sugar test, not "leaky gut syndrome."
- **CRF1 blockers** work in animals but have not become approved treatments (Taché & Million 2015). Do not suggest that a stress-hormone-blocking pill for IBS exists.
- **Stress changes the microbiome:** the strongest data are from mice (Bailey 2011). Grade claims about human stress and the microbiome as Preliminary.
- **"It's all in your head" framing:** avoid it. The book's message should be that stress-related gut symptoms involve measurable physiology (motility, permeability, mast cells) and real two-way brain-gut signaling (Koloski 2012).

### (c) Watch out for

- **"94% of IBS patients are hypersensitive" (Mertz 1995)** comes from one center and counts any altered perception, including referral patterns. Larger later cohorts show that hypersensitivity is common but not universal (Farmer & Aziz 2013 call it "not universal"). Suggested wording: "many, but not all, people with IBS."
- **Vagal vs spinal roles:** textbooks often say "vagus = nausea and fullness, spinal = pain." Brookes 2013 and Gebhart 2016 support overlapping roles. Say spinal afferents are the main pain pathway from the colon (Grundy 2019 focuses on spinal afferents) and the vagus carries mostly non-painful signals, and avoid absolute statements.
- **Brain regions:** Tillisch 2011 names the thalamus, insula and **anterior midcingulate** as common to everyone, and the **pregenual** anterior cingulate and amygdala as more active in IBS. Keep these sub-regions distinct and don't just write "ACC lights up in IBS."
- **Descending modulation evidence** (Wilder-Smith 2004) comes from 20 people. Grade it Preliminary.
- **Referred-pain maps** (for example, appendix pain starting near the navel) were not verified against a specific source in this dossier. Gebhart 2016 supports only the general principle. Verify any specific map before printing it.
- **"Chronic visceral pain affects >20%"** is a review-level estimate (Grundy 2019) and includes pelvic and bladder pain, not just the gut.

### (c) Watch out for

- **Three different "US IBD" numbers exist. They measure different things, so don't mix them up.**
  - Lewis 2023 (claims data, physician-diagnosed, all ages): **721/100,000, about 2.39 million (~0.7%)**. This is the best "how many Americans have IBD" figure.
  - CDC NHIS 2015 (self-reported "ever told," adults): **1.3%, 3.1 million**.
  - NCHS 2023-2024 (self-reported "ever told," adults): **1.6%, about 4.1 million**. Self-report counts anyone ever told they had UC or CD. That can include past misdiagnoses or confusion with other "colitis," so it runs higher than claims-based estimates. Use Lewis 2023 as the headline figure and mention the CDC survey as a second method.
- **The "~100k pediatric" figure comes from Kappelman 2025 (published online November 2024), not from Lewis 2023.** The Lewis 2023 abstract gives only the all-ages figure. Pediatric here means **under age 20**. Ye 2020 used ages 2-17 and found 77/100,000 in 2016 (1 in 1,299 children), a lower number because of the different age band, year and data source.
- **NIDDK figures are older:** UC 600,000-900,000 and Crohn's about 1 million. They don't match Lewis 2023 or NCHS 2026, so don't combine them in one sentence.
- **NCHS 2026 vs MMWR 2016:** the rise from 1.3% to 1.6% shouldn't be presented as a confirmed trend. NHIS was redesigned in 2019, and the 2026 report says its cross-sectional data cannot show change over time.
- **Approval years are by indication.** Many drugs were first approved for other diseases (for example ustekinumab for psoriasis in 2009, ozanimod for MS in 2020, guselkumab for psoriasis in 2017). Give the year the drug was approved *for UC or Crohn's*.
- **Certolizumab pegol (Crohn's, 2008) and natalizumab (Crohn's, 2008):** a search snippet gave these dates, but **no primary source was opened**. Leave them out or verify them before use.
- **5-ASA (sulfasalazine, mesalamine) and corticosteroid approval years were not verified.** Describe these as long-established older therapies, without years.
- **Obefazimod:** do not call it approved. As of the Abivax release of July 30, 2026, the NDA had not been submitted.
- **Tulisokibart (anti-TL1A)** and other pipeline drugs are investigational. Don't list them as treatments.
- **Ustekinumab pediatric UC date:** the FDA page gives April 15, 2026 for pediatric Crohn's but no exact date for pediatric UC (news coverage is dated late August 2026). Write "2026."
- **Mirikizumab Crohn's date:** news reports say January 15-16, 2025. Write "January 2025."
- **Vagus nerve stimulation** for Crohn's is experimental (7-patient pilot). Grade it Preliminary.
- **Anxiety/depression and IBD:** Barberio 2021 measured symptoms on screening questionnaires, not diagnoses, and cannot show which comes first.

### (c) Watch out for

- **Rome V is out (2026).** Any chapter text saying "Rome IV is the current standard" is out of date. Suggested approach: teach Rome IV as the version most published research used (including the 4.1% and 6.1% figures), then give the Rome V update in a box. **Prevalence figures are tied to the criteria version.** Always say "by Rome IV criteria" next to 4.1% or 6.1%.
- **Rome V IBS wording to use:** recurrent, but not continuous, abdominal pain or discomfort, on average at least 3 days per month in the last 3 months, with 2 or more of the three features. Onset at least 6 months before diagnosis. The 3 days per month threshold and "discomfort" resemble Rome III. Don't call them brand-new ideas.
- **"Continuous" pain:** Rome V excludes pain present all waking hours every day, which points toward centrally mediated abdominal pain syndrome instead. A 2026 clarification letter exists (Lembo et al., *Gastroenterology*, PMID 42468783; only its title and record were checked).
- **Rome V prevalence of 8.5%:** this comes from Krause & Chang's review. The underlying study wasn't opened. Report it as "early estimates suggest roughly double," or leave it out.
- **The 6.1% US figure (Almario 2023, PMID 37595647, confirmed)** came from an online survey during the COVID-19 pandemic, and the authors themselves raise this caveat. Earlier US Rome IV estimates were 4.7-5.3%.
- **NIDDK's "about 12% of Americans have IBS"** dates from the Rome III era. The page was last reviewed in November 2017. Don't use it as the current figure, or label it as an older, broader estimate.
- **Sperber 2021's 4.1%** comes from internet surveys in 26 countries. Household interviews gave 1.5%. Say "about 4% of adults in internet surveys across 26 countries."
- **No 2025 ACG IBS guideline was found.** The 2021 ACG guideline is still the ACG document. AGA published IBS-D and IBS-C pharmacologic guidelines in 2022 (the IBS-C one was not opened here). ACG did publish new UC and Crohn's guidelines in 2025 (Ch 9). Don't confuse the two.
- **Peppermint oil:** effective, but the evidence quality is very low (Ingrosso 2022). Grade it Moderate, not Strong.
- **TCAs for IBS are used at low doses as gut-brain neuromodulators,** not to treat depression. Say this explicitly so readers don't conclude "IBS is depression."
- **The low-FODMAP diet is a short elimination followed by reintroduction,** not a permanent diet. Most trials didn't test the reintroduction phase (Black 2022).

### Rome IV IBS criteria (wording)

Recurrent abdominal pain on average at least 1 day/week in the last 3 months, associated with two or more of the following: (1) related to defecation; (2) associated with a change in frequency of stool; (3) associated with a change in form (appearance) of stool. Criteria fulfilled for the last 3 months with symptom onset at least 6 months prior to diagnosis.
Source: [Rome Foundation, Rome IV criteria](https://theromefoundation.org/rome-iv/rome-iv-criteria/); Mearin et al., *Gastroenterology* 2016 ([PubMed 27144627](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27144627/)).

### Rome V IBS criteria (wording, 2026)

Recurrent, but not continuous, abdominal pain or discomfort on average at least 3 days per month in the last 3 months, associated with two or more of: related to defecation; change in frequency of stool; change in form (appearance) of stool. Criteria fulfilled for the last 3 months with symptom onset at least 6 months prior to diagnosis. Supportive: pain/discomfort should not be only related to menses.
Source: [Rome Foundation, Rome V criteria](https://theromefoundation.org/rome-v-criteria/); Corsetti et al., *Gastroenterology* 2026;170(6):1261-1282 ([PubMed 41713703](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41713703/)).
