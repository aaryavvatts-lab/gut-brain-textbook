    Frequently Asked Questions¶

    These are the questions readers ask most often about the gut-brain axis, gut conditions, and the health claims that fill social media. Each answer is short and links to the section of the book that explains it in full. Nothing here is medical advice. If you have symptoms or questions about your own care, talk to your doctor.

    Getting Started¶

    What is the gut-brain axis?¶

    The gut-brain axis is the two-way communication system that links the gut (its nervous system, hormones, immune cells, and microbes) with the brain. Signals travel both up and down, and nerves are only one route; hormones, immune messengers, and chemicals made by gut microbes carry messages too. See Chapter 1, Section 1.3.

    Who is this book for, and how much biology do I need?¶

    The book is written for high school students, teens living with a gut condition, their families and friends, and teachers. A year of high school biology is plenty. When a chapter needs basic neuron or brain ideas, it gives a short refresher and links to the matching chapter of the free companion book From Neuron to Bedside, as in Chapter 1, Section 1.3. Every chapter also lists its prerequisites at the top, and How to Use This Book suggests a path for each kind of reader.

    What is the Hype Meter?¶

    The Hype Meter is this book's four-level scale for grading how well the evidence supports a popular gut-brain claim: Strong, Moderate, Preliminary, or Hype. Two rules make it work: grade the claim exactly as it is stated, not the general topic, and remember that grades can change as new studies come out. A Strong grade for a treatment never means it is right for you personally. See Chapter 1, Section 1.12.

    What are the six questions for reading a gut-brain claim?¶

    Every chapter asks the same six questions about a real study: Mouse or human? Correlation or cause? How many? How long? Who paid for it? Is something being sold? You can use them on any headline, ad, or post, and on well-run studies too, because even a great study answers only the question it actually asked. See Chapter 1, Section 1.13.

    Who are Riya and Sam?¶

    Riya and Sam are fictional 16-year-olds who appear throughout the book. Riya was diagnosed with Crohn's disease at 14 and now takes a biologic medicine, and Sam, her best friend, has IBS with mixed bowel habits and test anxiety. Their stories open many chapters, starting with Chapter 1, Section 1.1 and Chapter 2, Section 2.1.

    Who is Vee?¶

    Vee is the book's guide, a cartoon fiber of the vagus nerve. The vagus nerve is the biggest nerve connection between the gut and the brainstem, so Vee carries the book's messages and pops up with tips, warnings, and key ideas. You can learn what Vee's real-life nerve does in Chapter 2, Section 2.2.

    What are the MicroSims, and how much can I trust them?¶

    Each chapter has a MicroSim, a small interactive simulation you can run in your browser, such as the vagus signal router in Chapter 2, Section 2.12. Every MicroSim is followed by a "What this model leaves out" box, because a simple model always skips parts of the real biology. Use the sims to build intuition, then check the details in the text.

    Two Nervous Systems, One Body¶

    Does my gut really have its own nervous system?¶

    Yes. The enteric nervous system is a network of neurons in the gut wall that runs reflexes such as peristalsis, the wave of muscle movement that pushes food along. Pieces of intestine cut off from the brain keep pushing their contents along; the brain adjusts gut activity but isn't needed for the basic reflex. See Chapter 1, Section 1.4 and Section 1.7.

    How many neurons are in the gut, and is it more than the brain?¶

    The enteric nervous system contains hundreds of millions of neurons, about 400 to 600 million by common estimates, roughly as many as the spinal cord. The brain has about 86 billion, well over a hundred times more, so "your gut has more neurons than your brain" earns a Hype grade. See Chapter 1, Section 1.5.

    Is the gut a "second brain"?¶

    "Second brain" is a useful nickname for a large, independent reflex network, but it is misleading if it suggests the gut thinks or feels. The enteric nervous system controls movement, secretion, and blood flow in the gut. Sensations like butterflies or nausea are created by the brain from signals the gut sends up. See Chapter 1, Section 1.11.

    What is the vagus nerve, and which way do its signals go?¶

    The vagus nerve is a long cranial nerve that connects the brainstem with the organs of the chest and abdomen, carrying signals in both directions. Most vagus nerve fibers carry signals from the body to the brain; classic fiber counts in animals put the figure at about 80%. That makes the vagus mainly a sensory cable, not just a "calm down" switch. See Chapter 2, Section 2.5.

    Is the vagus nerve the only way the gut talks to the brain?¶

    No. Spinal afferent nerves carry many gut sensations, including much of gut pain, and gut hormones and immune signals travel through the blood. The vagus is one important route among several. See Chapter 2, Section 2.8.

    How many microbes live in my body?¶

    A typical adult has roughly 38 trillion bacterial cells and 30 trillion human cells, about 1.3 to 1. The old "10 to 1" figure is wrong. Most gut bacteria are commensals that cause no harm, and many help, for example by fermenting fiber and crowding out germs that cause disease. See Chapter 3, Section 3.3.

    What happens to my gut microbes when I take antibiotics?¶

    Human studies show that antibiotics quickly reduce gut diversity, and recovery can be incomplete, with a few species missing for months in some people. But most of the community regrows within weeks to a couple of months in healthy adults, so "one round of antibiotics ruins your microbiome forever" is graded Hype. Antibiotics save lives, and that scary claim can push people to avoid ones they truly need. See Chapter 3, Section 3.8.

    Why do I feel so tired and low when I'm sick?¶

    That feeling is called sickness behavior, and it is triggered by real immune signals acting on the brain. In a randomized experiment, a dose of endotoxin (a bacterial molecule) lowered mood in healthy adults within hours. Immune messages reach the brain through the vagus nerve and other routes, even though the blood-brain barrier blocks many molecules. See Chapter 4, Section 4.10 and Section 4.8.

    How the Signals Travel¶

    Where is most of the body's serotonin made?¶

    More than 90% of the body's serotonin is made in the gut, mostly by enterochromaffin cells (some estimates say about 95%). Gut serotonin mainly runs gut movement, secretion, sensations like nausea, and platelet storage. See Chapter 5, Section 5.6.

    Can serotonin from my gut reach my brain and change my mood?¶

    No. Serotonin made in the gut does not cross the blood-brain barrier. The brain makes its own serotonin from tryptophan, an amino acid that can cross. That is why "90% of your serotonin is in your gut, so your gut controls your happiness" is graded Hype. See Chapter 5, Section 5.7 and Section 5.8.

    Why can an antidepressant like an SSRI upset the stomach?¶

    An SSRI blocks the serotonin transporter, so serotonin stays outside cells longer. A pill reaches the whole body, including the gut, where these transporters sit on lining cells, so gut serotonin lingers near the nerve endings that control gut movement and send nausea signals. See Chapter 5, Section 5.9.

    What does fiber have to do with the gut-brain axis?¶

    Fiber is the part of plant foods your own enzymes can't digest, so gut microbes ferment it into short-chain fatty acids: acetate (usually the most abundant), propionate, and butyrate. Butyrate is a main fuel for the cells lining the colon, and these molecules also send signals through receptors and genes. Average US intake is about 16 grams a day. See Chapter 6, Section 6.2 and Section 6.4.

    Is "leaky gut" real?¶

    Intestinal permeability is real and can be measured in research, mostly with sugar tests, and it rises in some diseases and stressful states. But "leaky gut syndrome" isn't a recognized diagnosis, and two independent research teams found that common commercial zonulin kits measure other proteins instead. The claim that leaky gut causes autism, depression, and everything else is graded Hype. See Chapter 6, Section 6.8.

    What happens in my body when I'm stressed?¶

    Stress works through two systems. The fast lane is fight-or-flight: the sympathetic nervous system and adrenaline prepare the body within seconds. The slow lane is the HPA axis, a hormone chain in which the hypothalamus signals the pituitary, the pituitary signals the adrenal glands, and the adrenal glands release cortisol, which also switches the system back off through negative feedback. See Chapter 7, Section 7.3 and Section 7.4.

    Why does my stomach act up before a big test or race?¶

    Fight-or-flight holds back the upper gut, but the stress hormone CRH can speed up the colon. In a small 1998 study of 10 people with IBS and 10 healthy volunteers, CRH increased movement in the lower colon in both groups, more in the people with IBS. A stress-related symptom is a real body event, not something imaginary. See Chapter 7, Section 7.8.

    Why can gut pain be so strong when tests come back normal?¶

    Pain is built by the nervous system, and its "volume" can be turned up in the gut's sensors, the spinal cord, or the brain. Human balloon studies since 1973 show that, on average, people with IBS feel gut stretch as painful at lower levels (55% vs 6% at one volume in the first study). Normal tests look for structural damage, not for how sensitive the pain system is. See Chapter 8, Section 8.8.

    When the System Goes Wrong¶

    What is the difference between IBD and IBS?¶

    IBD (Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis) involves visible, measurable inflammation and damage in the gut wall, found on tests like fecal calprotectin and colonoscopy. IBS is a disorder of gut-brain interaction in which the gut looks normal on these tests. They are different conditions, IBS does not turn into IBD, and they are treated very differently. See Chapter 9, Common Misconceptions and Chapter 10, Section 10.3.

    How are Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis different?¶

    Ulcerative colitis affects only the colon and rectum, starting at the rectum in one continuous stretch, and stays in the inner layers of the gut wall. Crohn's disease can affect any part of the digestive tract, most often the end of the small intestine and the start of the colon, often in patches. Crohn's inflammation is transmural, meaning it goes through the whole thickness of the wall, which is why it can cause strictures, fistulas, and abscesses. See Chapter 9, Section 9.3.

    How common is IBD in the US, including in kids and teens?¶

    Using insurance claims data, IBD is diagnosed in about 721 of every 100,000 Americans (about 0.7%), roughly 2.39 million people. Among children and teens under 20, the rate is about 122 per 100,000, roughly 100,000 young people. A national survey gives a higher number: in 2023 to 2024, 1.6% of US adults (about 4.1 million) said a doctor had told them they had Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis, because claims and surveys measure different things. See Chapter 9, Section 9.4.

    What causes IBD?¶

    Nobody knows exactly. Researchers think IBD develops when a person with a genetic tendency meets certain environmental exposures, and the immune system's relationship with gut microbes goes off track. Risk factors with good human evidence include family history and genes (such as NOD2 for Crohn's) and smoking, which may double the chance of developing Crohn's disease. Stress and "bad diet" are not on that list, and nobody causes their own IBD. See Chapter 9, IBD risk factors.

    How is IBD treated?¶

    Treatment has two jobs: induction (calming an active flare) and maintenance (keeping remission going). Medicine classes include aminosalicylates, corticosteroids, immunomodulators, biologics, and pills such as JAK inhibitors; medicines do not cure Crohn's disease, but they can bring on and maintain remission. Care teams aim for mucosal healing, not just feeling better. Treatment choices belong to a person and their care team. See Chapter 9, Section 9.8 and Section 9.9.

    What is IBS, and how is it diagnosed?¶

    IBS is a common disorder of gut-brain interaction marked by recurring belly pain linked to bowel movements and changes in stool, without visible damage. Most published research used the Rome IV criteria (2016), and by Rome IV criteria, 6.1% of US adults had IBS in a 2020 online survey. Rome V, published in May 2026, is now the current version. When there are no alarm features, doctors can make a confident positive diagnosis from the symptom pattern plus a few targeted tests. See Chapter 10, Section 10.4 and Section 10.6.

    What are alarm features?¶

    Alarm features are symptoms, signs, or history that suggest a disease other than IBS and call for more medical checking. NIDDK lists anemia, bleeding from the rectum or bloody or black, tarry stools, unplanned weight loss, and a family history of celiac disease, colon cancer, or inflammatory bowel disease. See Chapter 10, Alarm features.

    Are anxiety and depression more common in people with gut conditions?¶

    Yes. A 2021 meta-analysis of 77 studies of 30,118 adults with IBD found anxiety symptoms in 32.1% and depression symptoms in 25.2%, higher during active disease. For IBS, a 2019 meta-analysis found anxiety symptoms in 39.1% and depression symptoms in 28.8%. These are symptom questionnaires, not diagnoses. See Chapter 11, Section 11.4.

    Which comes first, the gut problem or the mood problem?¶

    It can run both ways. Studies show that anxiety in people who already have IBD predicts later flares, and active IBD predicts later anxiety. A link can also come from a third factor, such as poor sleep or pain. See Chapter 11, Section 11.5 and Section 11.6.

    What is celiac disease, and is it a food allergy?¶

    Celiac disease is a lifelong, immune-driven disorder in which eating gluten (proteins in wheat, barley, and rye) damages the lining of the small intestine. It affects about 1% of people worldwide. It is not a classic food allergy: it doesn't involve IgE, the antibody behind immediate allergic reactions, and wheat allergy is a separate condition. See Chapter 12, Section 12.3 and Section 12.9.

    What does the research say about autism and the gut?¶

    Autistic children have more gut symptoms on average: a 2014 meta-analysis found higher odds of general GI symptoms (about 4.4 times). These are real health problems worth treating with standard care, separate from autism itself. A 2021 study of 247 children and teens found that diet differences, linked to selective eating, explained most of the microbiome differences, so the evidence does not show that gut bacteria cause autism. See Chapter 13, Section 13.3 and Section 13.5.

    What Can Actually Help¶

    Can what I eat affect my mood?¶

    Research studies whole eating patterns, not single foods. Large observational studies link more ultra-processed food with more depression and anxiety symptoms (Moderate for the association; cause unproven). In the SMILES trial, 67 adults with major depression were randomized for 12 weeks, and remission was 32.3% in the diet-support group versus 8.0% in the social-support group, but the trial was small and short, participants knew their group, and diet was an add-on to usual care. See Chapter 15, Section 15.2 and The SMILES trial.

    How much fiber do teens eat compared with what guidelines suggest?¶

    Average US intake is about 16 grams a day. The 2020 to 2025 Dietary Guidelines suggested about 25 grams for girls 14 to 18 and 31 grams for boys 14 to 18. The current 2025 to 2030 Dietary Guidelines give no gram target; they emphasize high-fiber, minimally processed foods. See Chapter 15, The fiber gap.

    What is the difference between a probiotic and a prebiotic?¶

    A probiotic is "live microorganisms that, when given in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit on the host." A prebiotic is "a substrate that is selectively utilized by host microorganisms conferring a health benefit," which is picky food for your microbes. Both definitions require a proven health benefit, the part marketing tends to skip. See Chapter 16, Section 16.2.

    Do probiotics treat depression or anxiety?¶

    The evidence is Preliminary. A 2024 review of 12 trials with 707 people found improvement on one depression scale (BDI) but not on three others (HAMD, DASS, MADRS). "Psychobiotic" is a research term coined in 2013, not an approved product category, and no probiotic is FDA approved to treat any mental health condition. See Chapter 16, Section 16.8.

    Does a bigger CFU number mean a better probiotic?¶

    No. CFU counts live microbes that can multiply, but what matters is the specific strain, at the dose shown to work in a study, for the condition you care about. A count printed "at time of manufacture" may be much higher than what is still alive when you open the bottle. See Chapter 16, Section 16.4.

    What is a fecal microbiota transplant, and what is it approved for?¶

    A fecal microbiota transplant (FMT) delivers stool from a carefully screened healthy donor into a patient's gut to restore a healthier microbial community. It works well for recurrent C. difficile infection. Two stool-based products are FDA approved: Rebyota (November 30, 2022, rectal) and Vowst (April 26, 2023, oral capsules), both to prevent C. difficile infection from coming back in adults 18 and older after antibiotic treatment for recurrent infection. In the Vowst trial, recurrence was 12.4% versus 39.8% with placebo at 8 weeks. See Chapter 17, Section 17.3 and Section 17.4.

    What is vagus nerve stimulation approved for?¶

    Implanted vagus nerve stimulators are FDA approved for drug-resistant partial-onset epilepsy (1997, add-on), for chronic or recurrent treatment-resistant depression in adults (2005, add-on, after 4 or more failed treatments), and, as the SetPoint System, for moderate to severe rheumatoid arthritis in adults who didn't respond to biologic or targeted drugs (July 30, 2025). The handheld gammaCore device is FDA authorized for cluster headache (2017) and migraine (2018) in adults. No VNS device is approved for Crohn's disease, IBS, or anxiety. One separate ear device, IB-Stim, was authorized by the FDA in 2019 to help reduce IBS belly pain in patients 11 to 18 years old. See Chapter 17, Section 17.7 and Section 17.8.

    Why would a doctor prescribe an antidepressant for IBS?¶

    Low doses of tricyclic antidepressants are used as gut-brain neuromodulators that turn down pain signaling along the gut-brain axis, not to treat depression. In the 2023 ATLANTIS trial, amitriptyline improved IBS symptoms without evidence of changing depression scores. It does not mean the doctor thinks the pain is "all in your head." See Chapter 17, Section 17.9.

    Can therapy help a gut condition like IBS?¶

    Yes. Brain-gut psychotherapies such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and gut-directed hypnotherapy work on the brain's side of the gut-brain loop. A 2020 network meta-analysis of 41 trials with 4,072 people found they reduced the chance of ongoing IBS symptoms, graded Moderate (near the top of the range) because many trials had a high risk of bias. They help many people but don't cure IBS. See Chapter 18, Section 18.8.

    How much sleep do teens need, and does it matter for the gut?¶

    The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends 8 to 10 hours per 24 hours for ages 13 to 18, but in 2025 only 24% of US high school students reported at least 8 hours on an average school night. Disturbed sleep is linked with slightly higher inflammation markers, and in one study, people with Crohn's disease in remission who reported poor sleep had about twice the odds of later active disease. That is an association, not proof of cause. See Chapter 18, How much sleep teens need and Section 18.4.

    Does exercise help with mood and gut symptoms?¶

    The mood benefit is Strong: a 2023 umbrella review covering more than 1,000 randomized trials with over 128,000 adults found medium-sized improvements in depression and anxiety symptoms. Regular moderate activity improved IBS symptoms in a randomized trial, but very long, hard exercise can trigger gut symptoms. The claim that exercise improves mood by changing the microbiome is graded Hype: exercise does change gut microbes, but no human study has shown that those changes are why exercise helps mood. See Chapter 18, Section 18.7.

    Living With It¶

    Why is it so hard to talk about bowel symptoms?¶

    Gut conditions involve body functions many cultures treat as private, which sets up stigma; in one survey of 211 adults with IBD, 84% reported feeling stigmatized. Hiding a condition (illness concealment) makes sense in the moment, but if teachers, friends, or doctors don't know, they can't help. Bowel symptoms are normal medical information. See Chapter 19, Section 19.3.

    What school accommodations can help a student with a gut condition?¶

    In the US, a 504 plan lists the accommodations a student with a disability needs for equal access to education, and IBD can qualify. Commonly requested accommodations include unlimited restroom access, "stop-the-clock" testing, and making up missed work without penalty. A student doesn't have to be failing to qualify, and students with IBS and other conditions can also ask; the school decides case by case. See Chapter 19, Section 19.8.

    Does IBD only happen in Western countries?¶

    No. A 2017 review of 147 studies found rising IBD in newly industrialized countries in Asia, Africa, and South America. IBD became common first in Western countries and is now rising in many other parts of the world. See Chapter 19, Section 19.5.

    How can I get more out of a doctor's visit?¶

    Before the visit, write down your questions and symptoms, and list every medicine, vitamin, herb, and supplement you take. Be truthful, even about embarrassing symptoms, and bring a trusted person if it helps. For treatment decisions, useful questions include: What are my options? What are the likely benefits and harms? Was this tested in people like me? See Chapter 19, Section 19.7 and Chapter 17, Section 17.12.

    How can I check a health claim I see online?¶

    Use the book's claim-checking method: pin down the exact claim, trace it to the original source, ask the six questions, check the numbers, see whether reviews and other studies agree, grade it, and rewrite it honestly. One study rarely settles anything. See Chapter 20, Section 20.12.

    What is the difference between relative risk and absolute risk?¶

    Relative risk compares groups ("cuts your risk by 50%"), while absolute risk tells you how many people are actually affected. If the starting risk is tiny, a big relative cut may help very few people. Always look for the absolute numbers, such as 12% versus 40%. See Chapter 20, Section 20.8.

    Common Myths¶

    Is 70% of your immune system in your gut?¶

    No. The gut holds about 70% of the body's antibody-making plasma cells, but only about 3% of all immune cells, according to a careful 2023 count. The popular "70% of your immune system" claim is graded Hype and is often used to sell gut supplements. See Chapter 4, The "70%" number.

    Is depression caused by low serotonin?¶

    That slogan is graded Hype. A 2022 umbrella review found no consistent evidence that depression is caused by lowered serotonin, and depression has many causes. This is a separate question from whether antidepressants help. See Chapter 5, Section 5.10.

    Do foods with serotonin, like bananas, raise brain serotonin?¶

    No. Serotonin in food can't cross the blood-brain barrier any better than gut serotonin can. See Chapter 5, Common Misconceptions.

    Does stress cause IBD?¶

    No good evidence shows that stress causes IBD to start. NIDDK lists immune, genetic, environmental, and microbial factors as possible causes of Crohn's disease, not stress. In people who already have IBD, high perceived stress is linked to more flares, which is a different and narrower claim. See Chapter 7, Section 7.10.

    Is IBS just anxiety, or "all in your head"?¶

    No. Anxiety is more common in IBS, but a diagnosed anxiety disorder was found in about 23% of people with IBS, so most don't have one. IBS involves measurable changes in gut sensitivity and movement and can start after a gut infection. Pain always involves the brain, but that makes it real, not imaginary. See Chapter 10, Section 10.10 and Chapter 8, Section 8.13.

    Is a gluten-free diet healthier for everyone?¶

    For people with celiac disease it is essential. But in people without celiac disease, a 2021 study of 13,494 women found no link between gluten intake and memory and thinking scores. Cutting gluten without a reason can cost more, complicate diagnosis, and narrow the diet. See Chapter 12, "Gluten is poison for everyone's brain".

    Should a low-FODMAP diet be followed forever?¶

    No, that claim is graded Hype. The low-FODMAP diet is designed as a short elimination phase, best done with a dietitian, followed by adding foods back one group at a time so people avoid only the foods that bother them. Cutting out many foods for good can make eating stressful and limit the variety of foods that gut microbes use. See Chapter 10, The low-FODMAP diet.

    Do fecal transplants cure autism?¶

    No. The best-known study, from 2017, treated 18 autistic children aged 7 to 16 in an open-label trial with no placebo group, so it can't show cause. A 2024 placebo-controlled trial of 103 autistic children found no significant difference from placebo on its main behavior measure. Autism is a lifelong neurodevelopmental difference, not a disease to cure. See Chapter 13, The microbiota transfer therapy studies.

    Can a home microbiome test tell me what to eat to feel better?¶

    That claim is graded Hype. Healthy people have very different gut communities, scientists haven't defined a single healthy microbiome, and a 2024 international expert consensus said direct-to-consumer tests have no proven clinical value. See Chapter 3, Section 3.10 and Section 3.11.

    Do cold plunges, humming, or gargling "reset" your vagus nerve?¶

    That claim is graded Hype. Small lab studies show brief cold or slow breathing can briefly shift heart rate and heart rate variability, which are indirect estimates of vagal activity at the heart. None of the studies show these tricks "reset" the vagus nerve, change gut function, or treat an anxiety disorder. See Chapter 2, Section 2.13.

    Does removing your appendix prevent Parkinson's disease?¶

    No. One 2018 study found a lower risk, but a large Danish study found a slightly higher risk, and two meta-analyses found no overall link. Appendix removal is not a prevention strategy. See Chapter 14, The appendix puzzle.

    For Families¶

    My teen was just diagnosed with IBD. Did we cause it?¶

    No. IBD develops from a combination of genes, an overactive immune response, gut microbes, and environmental factors such as smoking. No good evidence shows that stress or diet starts the disease, and nobody causes their own IBD. See Chapter 9, IBD risk factors and Chapter 9, Common Misconceptions.

    What should we avoid saying to a teen with a gut condition?¶

    Avoid suggesting that worrying caused their condition, because that isn't what the science shows and it adds guilt. Instead of "You just need to stay positive," try something like "You've seemed down lately. Do you want to talk, or should we bring it up with your doctor?" See the family notes in Chapter 11, Section 11.11 and the For Families page.

    How can we tell if a gut condition is affecting our teen's mood?¶

    A teen who seems more irritable, withdrawn, or tired than usual may be dealing with the illness, mood, or both, and it's worth asking gently. Anxiety and depression symptoms are common with gut conditions, especially during flares. You can help by mentioning mood at gut appointments and by learning the warning signs of depression and the 988 Lifeline yourself. See Chapter 11, Section 11.11.

    A relative keeps recommending a home remedy or supplement. What do we say?¶

    You can say, "We're going to check that with the care team," which respects the relative and protects your child. No trial has shown that ghee, or any single food from any culture, heals Crohn's disease, and home "fecal transplants" or vagus gadgets carry real risks or lack evidence. See the family notes in Chapter 17, Section 17.12 and Chapter 19, Section 19.1.

    How can we help at medical appointments?¶

    Help your teen ask questions instead of answering for them, and let them speak first. Bring a list of questions, such as what the options are, what the likely benefits and harms are, and whether a use is approved, off-label, or experimental. Shared decision-making combines the clinician's knowledge with your teen's own values and goals. See Chapter 17, Section 17.12.

    Are probiotics safe for babies?¶

    Not always. In October 2023, the FDA warned that probiotics given to preterm infants have caused serious infections, including a death, and no probiotic is FDA approved as a drug for infants. Expert groups have disagreed on this question, so decisions belong to a baby's care team. See Chapter 16, Section 16.6.

    For Teachers¶

    How can I use this book in a biology or health class?¶

    Every chapter follows the same pattern: a hook, an everyday comparison, numbered science sections, Hype Meter cards, a MicroSim, a fictional At the Bedside case, Common Misconceptions, a Your Gut Check practice set, and 12 exercises across Bloom's Taxonomy from Remember to Create. Each chapter also has a 10-question quiz with explanations. See the Exercises in Chapter 1 for an example, and the For Teachers page.

    How can I teach students to evaluate health claims?¶

    Start with the Hype Meter and the six questions in Chapter 1, which every chapter applies to a real study in a "How to read a gut-brain claim" box. Chapter 20 pulls it together with the hierarchy of evidence, absolute versus relative risk, conflicts of interest, and a step-by-step claim-checking method. See Chapter 1, Section 1.13 and Chapter 20, Section 20.12.

    Is there a ready-made student project?¶

    Yes. Chapter 20 includes a student research project template: students pick one health claim, find and evaluate the original evidence, fill in a six-question table, assign a Hype Meter grade, and explain the result in plain language for a real audience. See Chapter 20, Section 20.14.

    How should I talk about autism and ADHD in class?¶

    The book describes autism and ADHD as neurodevelopmental differences, part of normal human diversity, not diseases to fix. It uses identity-first language ("autistic people") as the default, and it treats GI problems in autistic people as real health issues worth treating, separate from autism itself. See Chapter 13, Section 13.2 and Words matter: identity-first language.

    How can I support a student with IBD or IBS in my class?¶

    A 504 plan can include accommodations such as unlimited restroom access and stop-the-clock testing. These aren't unfair advantages; they remove barriers so students can learn. The student should help choose the accommodations, because someday they'll need to advocate for themselves. See Chapter 19, Section 19.8.

    Using This Book Safely¶

    Is this book medical advice?¶

    No. This book teaches science, and its cases are fictional teaching cases. It never tells readers to start, stop, or change a medicine, diet, or supplement. If you or someone you know has symptoms, talk to a doctor. See the note at the end of any At the Bedside case, such as Chapter 1, At the Bedside.

    What should I do if I'm struggling or thinking about suicide?¶

    If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, call or text 988 to reach the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (in the US), chat at 988lifeline.org, or go to the nearest emergency room. It is free, confidential, and available 24 hours a day, 7 days a week. If someone is in immediate danger, call 911. Outside the US, you can find a helpline at findahelpline.com, and you can also talk to a parent, school counselor, or doctor. See Chapter 11, Help right now.

    Can I use the Rome criteria to diagnose myself or a friend?¶

    Please don't. Matching the symptom pattern isn't enough without a clinician's history, exam, alarm-feature check, and the right tests, because conditions like IBD and celiac disease can look similar at first. The Rome Criteria Explorer is for learning how diagnosis works, not for diagnosing anyone. See Chapter 10, Section 10.6 and Section 10.7.

    Should I go gluten-free before getting tested for celiac disease?¶

    NIDDK says doctors don't recommend starting a gluten-free diet before testing, because removing gluten can make the blood test and biopsy look normal even in someone with celiac disease. Anyone who has already gone gluten-free should talk with their doctor about testing. See Chapter 12, Why "go gluten-free first" is a trap.

    Has the FDA checked that supplements work?¶

    No. Under a 1994 law, the FDA does not approve dietary supplements, including most probiotics, before they are sold, and companies are responsible for their own safety and labels. Labels may make structure-function claims like "supports digestive health" without FDA approval, but they can't claim to treat, cure, or prevent a disease. See Chapter 16, Section 16.5 and Chapter 20, Section 20.10.

    Is a do-it-yourself fecal transplant safe?¶

    No. Stool can carry viruses, parasites, and drug-resistant germs, and in 2019 a drug-resistant E. coli infection traced to one FMT donor killed a patient, even with screening. Medical FMT depends on donor screening, stool testing, and follow-up; homemade versions skip all of them. See Chapter 17, Why screening matters, and why DIY FMT is dangerous.

    What if healthy-eating goals start turning into strict food rules?¶

    Strict food rules, cutting out whole food groups, or an intense focus on eating "perfectly" can be warning signs of eating disorder risk. The food and mood trials in this book added variety and support; none tested cutting out food groups to improve mood. If you notice stricter and stricter rules or guilt after eating, that's worth talking about with a doctor or counselor. See Chapter 15, Section 15.9.
