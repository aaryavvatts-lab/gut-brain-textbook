    4. 12. Celiac Disease and Food Reactions

    Chapter 12: Celiac Disease and Food Reactions¶

    Summary¶

    Celiac disease is a lifelong immune reaction to gluten that damages the tiny, finger-like villi lining the small intestine, and it affects about 1 in 100 people worldwide. This chapter explains why only people with certain gene variants get it, how a blood test and a small bowel biopsy confirm it, why the gluten-free diet is the only proven treatment, and how celiac disease can reach the nervous system. It then sorts out the confusing family of food reactions: food allergy, food intolerance (such as lactose intolerance), and non-celiac gluten sensitivity, where blinded trials suggest a carbohydrate called fructan may often be the real trigger. Along the way you will see why cutting out foods without a reason can backfire.

    Not every food reaction is the same reaction

    Gluten-free labels are everywhere, and almost everyone knows someone who "can't eat" some food. Some of those reactions are serious immune diseases, some are digestion problems, and some are still a scientific puzzle. By the end of this chapter you'll be able to tell them apart, and you'll know why the order of testing matters so much.

    12.1 The Gluten-Free Puzzle¶

    Sam's older cousin, the one who keeps posting about supplements, has a new announcement. He has gone gluten-free, and he says his "brain fog" is gone. His post ends with a link to a gluten-free snack brand and the line "Gluten is poison for everyone's brain." Sam rolls his eyes, but he also wonders. Gluten-free menus are at every restaurant in town. Is gluten actually bad for everyone? (Sam, Riya, and their friends are fictional students who appear throughout this book.)

    Meanwhile, Riya has a real reason to care about gluten. A girl in her orchestra section, Mei (also fictional), was diagnosed with celiac disease last year. When Riya and her grandmother bake for the orchestra potluck, Mei can't eat anything made with regular flour, and she can't even eat a gluten-free cookie if it was cut with the same knife as a regular one. For Mei, gluten is not a lifestyle choice. It is a trigger for an immune attack on her own intestine.

    Here is the puzzle. A 2018 review of 96 studies estimated that about 1 in 100 people worldwide has celiac disease. Yet in a US national survey, the share of people who avoided gluten without having celiac disease more than tripled in just a few years, from 0.5% in 2009 to 2010 to 1.7% in 2013 to 2014, according to a 2016 analysis by Choung and colleagues. By then, more people were avoiding gluten without a diagnosis than had celiac disease at all (about 0.7% of people in the same survey). So who actually needs to avoid gluten, and why? To answer that, we need to look at the lining of the small intestine up close.

    12.2 A Carpet That Feeds You: The Intestinal Villi¶

    Picture two bath mats. One is a flat rubber mat. The other is a thick, shaggy towel-like mat with thousands of soft loops. Spill a glass of water on each. The flat mat barely holds any; the shaggy one soaks it up, because all those loops add up to a huge surface. Your small intestine is built like the shaggy mat.

    Intestinal villi are tiny, finger-like projections of the small intestine's lining that greatly increase its surface area for absorbing nutrients. According to the open textbook Anatomy and Physiology 2e from OpenStax, each villus is only about 0.5 to 1 millimeter long, and there are about 20 to 40 of them in every square millimeter of lining. Inside each villus is a tiny bed of blood capillaries and a lymph vessel, which carry absorbed nutrients away.

    The surface gets even bigger at a smaller scale. Each absorbing cell on a villus is covered with microvilli, hair-like extensions of the cell membrane about 1 micrometer long. Under a microscope, they look like the bristles of a brush, so together they are called the brush border. Enzymes stuck to the brush border finish digesting carbohydrates and proteins. One of those enzymes, lactase, breaks down the milk sugar lactose, which will matter in Section 12.9.

    OpenStax notes that the folds of the lining, the villi, and the microvilli together increase the small intestine's absorptive surface more than 600-fold compared with a smooth tube. That is the whole point: food is broken into small molecules, and those molecules must touch a cell to get into your body. More surface means more absorption.

    Level Structure Rough size What it adds
    Biggest Circular folds of the lining Ridges you can see with the naked eye Slow food down and add surface
    Middle Villi About 0.5 to 1 mm long Tens of villi per square millimeter
    Smallest Microvilli (brush border) About 1 micrometer Huge membrane surface plus digestive enzymes

    Now imagine someone mowing the shaggy mat down until it is nearly flat. The mat still exists, but it can't soak up much. That is what celiac disease does to the small intestine.

    12.3 What Celiac Disease Is¶

    Celiac disease is a lifelong, immune-driven disorder in which eating gluten triggers the immune system to damage the lining of the small intestine. The American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) 2023 guideline defines it as a permanent immune-mediated response to gluten. The National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK) calls it a chronic digestive and immune disorder. Many scientists describe it as autoimmune, meaning the immune system attacks the body's own tissue, because the immune reaction damages the person's own intestine and produces antibodies against one of the body's own enzymes (more on that in Section 12.4).

    Gluten is a group of proteins found naturally in wheat, barley, and rye. It gives bread dough its stretch. According to NIDDK, gluten is in obvious foods like bread, pasta, cookies, and cakes, but also in many processed foods, because ingredients such as some starches, flavorings, and malt are made from these grains.

    Who can get it: the gene "key"¶

    Here is a fact that surprises many people: you can't get celiac disease unless you carry certain gene variants. Think of it like a lock and key. The genes make a "display stand" on immune cells, and only some display stands hold gluten pieces in a way that sets off an alarm.

    HLA-DQ2 and HLA-DQ8 are versions (variants) of immune-system genes in the human leukocyte antigen (HLA) family that make proteins on certain immune cells; nearly everyone with celiac disease carries one or both. MedlinePlus Genetics, a US National Library of Medicine site, explains that HLA proteins hold up small protein fragments so other immune cells can check whether they are "self" or "foreign." In celiac disease, the immune system wrongly treats fragments of gluten as a threat.

    But carrying the key is not enough. NIDDK says about 30% of people have DQ2 or DQ8, yet only about 3% of those carriers develop celiac disease. A person also has to eat gluten, and other genes and environmental factors (such as early infections, which researchers are still studying) probably play a role. That means a genetic test is more useful for ruling celiac disease out than for ruling it in: if you don't carry DQ2 or DQ8, celiac disease is very unlikely.

    How common is it?¶

    Measure Figure Source
    Worldwide, positive blood test 1.4% (in 275,818 people) Singh et al. 2018 meta-analysis
    Worldwide, confirmed by biopsy 0.7% (in 138,792 people) Singh et al. 2018 meta-analysis
    North America, confirmed by biopsy 0.5% Singh et al. 2018
    United States, estimated number of people About 2 million NIDDK

    So "about 1 in 100 people worldwide" is a fair summary. Singh's review also found celiac disease more common in females than males (0.6% vs 0.4%) and in children than adults.

    NIDDK notes that many people with celiac disease have not been diagnosed, that some people have celiac disease along with other immune-related conditions such as type 1 diabetes or thyroid disease, and that it is more common in people with certain chromosome conditions such as Down syndrome. It also runs in families: MedlinePlus Genetics says parents, siblings, and children of someone with celiac disease have about a 4 to 15% chance of developing it.

    A gene that's necessary, not sufficient

    I like to think of DQ2 and DQ8 as a ticket to a concert: you can't get in without one, but holding a ticket doesn't mean you'll go. About 30% of people hold the ticket, and only about 3% of them ever get celiac disease.

    12.4 Villous Atrophy: What the Damage Looks Like¶

    What happens when someone with celiac disease eats gluten? Here is the chain of events in plain steps.

    1. Gluten arrives. Gluten proteins are tough, and some fragments survive digestion and reach the lining of the small intestine.
    2. An enzyme changes the fragments. An enzyme in the intestine called tissue transglutaminase (tTG) chemically modifies gluten fragments. A 2021 trial report by Schuppan and colleagues in the New England Journal of Medicine explains that this change makes the fragments stimulate immune T cells more strongly.
    3. The display stand shows them. Immune cells carrying DQ2 or DQ8 hold up the modified fragments, and T cells (white blood cells that direct immune attacks) react as if they had found an invader.
    4. Inflammation follows. The activated immune cells release inflammatory signals (cytokines, which you met in Chapter 4), and immune cells crowd into the lining.
    5. The villi shrink. The lining is damaged faster than it can rebuild. The villi get shorter and blunter, and in severe cases flatten almost completely.
    6. The body makes antibodies against itself. The immune system also makes antibodies against tTG, the body's own enzyme. That's part of why celiac disease is called autoimmune, and it is what the main blood test looks for.

    The result is villous atrophy, the shortening and flattening of the intestinal villi, seen under a microscope, that is the hallmark damage of celiac disease. MedlinePlus Genetics describes the villi in celiac disease becoming shortened and eventually flattening out. A 2018 Lancet review by Lebwohl, Sanders, and Green says the diagnosis requires villous atrophy in the first part of the small intestine (the duodenum).

    With less surface, the intestine absorbs less. That one fact explains many celiac symptoms, which NIDDK lists as including diarrhea, bloating, gas, belly pain, and greasy stools; in children, slow growth, weight loss, delayed puberty, and damage to tooth enamel; and in anyone, fatigue, anemia (low red blood cells, often from poor iron absorption), and weak bones. Damage to the brush border can also reduce lactase, so NIDDK notes that some people with celiac disease become lactose intolerant until the lining heals.

    Symptoms vary a lot. NIDDK says digestive symptoms are more common in children than adults, and some people have no obvious symptoms but still have damage. MedlinePlus Genetics notes that, on average, diagnosis comes 6 to 10 years after symptoms begin, partly because tiredness and belly pain have many other causes.

    12.5 Testing: The Blood Test and the Biopsy¶

    Because celiac symptoms overlap with IBS, lactose intolerance, and many other conditions, NIDDK says doctors don't diagnose celiac disease from symptoms alone. They use two main tests.

    Tissue transglutaminase antibody (tTG antibody) testing is a blood test that measures antibodies against tTG, the body's own intestinal enzyme; high levels suggest active celiac disease. The ACG 2023 guideline says tTG antibody testing is very helpful for the first screening of people suspected of having celiac disease. The standard tTG test measures antibodies of a type called IgA. NIDDK notes that selective IgA deficiency, in which the body makes little or no IgA, is more common in people with celiac disease, so doctors may check a person's overall IgA level along with the tTG test.

    A small bowel biopsy is the removal of tiny pieces of tissue from the small intestine, usually the duodenum, during an upper endoscopy, so a pathologist can look for villous atrophy and immune cells under a microscope. In an upper endoscopy, a doctor passes a thin, flexible tube with a camera through the mouth and stomach into the first part of the small intestine, according to NIDDK. The ACG guideline says a biopsy is required to confirm the diagnosis in most patients, although a no-biopsy route can be used for selected children who meet strict criteria.

    Under the 2020 European pediatric guidelines (ESPGHAN), this route can be used in children whose tTG antibody level is at least 10 times the upper limit of normal, confirmed by a second antibody test (endomysial antibodies) on a separate blood sample; symptoms are no longer required.

    Why "go gluten-free first" is a trap¶

    Both tests look for the immune system's response to gluten. Take the gluten away, and the response fades: antibodies drop and the villi start to heal. That's great for treatment, but terrible for diagnosis. NIDDK states plainly that doctors don't recommend starting a gluten-free diet before diagnostic testing, because the diet can affect test results.

    Think of a smoke detector test. If you want to know whether the detector works, you have to make smoke while you test it. If you clear the room first, the detector stays silent, and you learn nothing about whether there was ever a fire.

    Someone who has already gone gluten-free and wants to be tested should talk with their doctor about what to do. Reintroducing gluten for testing is a medical decision, so it should be planned with the care team, not done alone.

    Test first, then change the diet

    The most common mistake I see is someone cutting out gluten "just to see," feeling a bit better, and then getting a negative test months later. If you think gluten bothers you, the first step is a conversation with a doctor while you're still eating your usual diet.

    12.6 The Gluten-Free Diet: Simple to Say, Hard to Do¶

    The gluten-free diet is an eating plan that strictly avoids wheat, barley, rye, and ingredients made from them; for people with celiac disease it is lifelong and is the only proven treatment. The ACG 2023 guideline says treatment requires strict adherence to a gluten-free diet and lifelong medical follow-up, and that most patients respond very well. NIDDK says that for most people the diet heals the small intestine and prevents further damage, and many people notice symptoms improve within days to weeks.

    "Strict" really means strict. NIDDK describes several challenges:

    • Hidden gluten. Many additives, such as malt flavoring and some modified food starches, can come from gluten grains.
    • Cross-contact. A gluten-free food can pick up gluten from a shared toaster, cutting board, fryer oil, or knife, like Mei's cookie in Section 12.1.
    • Labels. In the United States, the FDA requires foods labeled "gluten-free" to contain less than 20 parts per million of gluten, an amount NIDDK says is too small to cause problems for most people with celiac disease.
    • Oats. NIDDK says research suggests most people with celiac disease can safely eat moderate amounts of oats, but oats are often contaminated with other grains, so this is a question for a doctor or dietitian.
    • Cost. NIDDK notes that packaged gluten-free foods tend to cost more than the regular versions.

    That's why NIDDK recommends a registered dietitian, a nutrition professional who can teach label reading, plan balanced meals, and check for nutrient gaps. Many foods are naturally gluten-free, including rice, potatoes, corn, beans, fruits, vegetables, meat, and fish, so the diet doesn't have to be bland. Riya's grandmother, who cooks a lot of rice and lentil dishes, discovers that many family recipes were gluten-free all along; the things to watch are spices or mixes that may contain wheat, and utensils that need to stay separate.

    Follow-up matters too. NIDDK says doctors may recheck antibody levels, which typically return to normal on the diet, and sometimes repeat a biopsy. About 20% of people with celiac disease have symptoms that continue or return on a gluten-free diet, according to NIDDK. Causes include small amounts of hidden gluten and other health problems. A rare form called refractory celiac disease doesn't heal even on a strict diet and needs specialist care.

    Healing is slower than feeling better. In a 2010 Mayo Clinic study of 241 adults with biopsy-proven celiac disease, Rubio-Tapia and colleagues found confirmed healing of the lining in about 34% by 2 years and about 66% by 5 years, and feeling better did not reliably predict whether the lining had healed.

    If you're going through this

    If you've just been diagnosed with celiac disease, it's normal to feel overwhelmed, or even to grieve foods you love. You're not being dramatic; food is tied to family, culture, and friendship. A dietitian can help you find safe versions of your favorite meals, and many teens find it easier once they have a few go-to snacks and a short script, like "I have celiac disease, so I can't share, but thanks." If you keep having symptoms on the diet, tell your care team instead of assuming you failed. Hidden gluten is common and fixable.

    12.7 Beyond the Gut: Celiac Disease and the Nervous System¶

    Celiac disease is not only a gut disease. The ACG guideline describes it as resembling a multisystem disorder. NIDDK lists nervous system symptoms such as headaches, balance problems, seizures, and peripheral neuropathy, as well as mental health problems such as depression or anxiety.

    Peripheral neuropathy is damage to the peripheral nerves (the nerves outside the brain and spinal cord), often causing numbness, tingling, burning, or weakness, usually starting in the hands and feet. You can picture it like a frayed phone cord: messages from the skin arrive garbled or not at all.

    Gluten ataxia is a problem with balance and coordination linked to gluten sensitivity, thought to involve immune damage to the cerebellum, the part of the brain that fine-tunes movement. (For a refresher on the cerebellum, see From Neuron to Bedside, Chapter 7.) "Ataxia" means unsteady, uncoordinated movement.

    How common are these? A 2019 systematic review by Mearns and colleagues pooled 16 studies of people with celiac disease. Neuropathy was reported in up to 39% of patients across 13 studies, while gluten ataxia was much less common, from 0% to 6% across 9 studies. The numbers vary widely because the studies were small and used different methods, so "up to 39%" is the top of a wide range, not a typical rate. The review also found that sticking to a gluten-free diet appeared to improve both conditions. Note that several authors of that review worked for or consulted for a drug company (Takeda), which the paper discloses.

    Neurological problems in people with celiac disease have been reported since 1966, according to a 2010 Lancet Neurology review by Hadjivassiliou and colleagues, but exactly how a gluten-triggered immune reaction leads to nerve damage is still being studied.

    What about mood? A 2012 meta-analysis by Smith and Gerdes of 18 depression studies found that depression was more common or more severe in adults with celiac disease than in healthy adults, but about the same as in adults with other long-term physical illnesses. Anxiety was not reliably different. That pattern suggests the link may come partly from the general stress of living with a chronic illness, not from something unique to gluten. It's a correlation, so it can't tell us the cause.

    Hype Meter: "Celiac disease can affect your nerves and brain, not just your gut"

    StrongModeratePreliminaryHype
    Verdict: Moderate. Human studies consistently link celiac disease with peripheral neuropathy (reported in up to 39% of patients across small studies) and, less often, gluten ataxia (0% to 6%), and NIDDK lists nervous system symptoms as part of the disease. But the studies are small and varied, the exact mechanism is unclear, and the evidence that the gluten-free diet helps comes from observation, not randomized trials. What would change the grade: Large studies that follow people from diagnosis with careful nerve testing, plus a clear mechanism, could move it to Strong.

    "Gluten is poison for everyone's brain"¶

    Sam's cousin made a much bigger claim: that gluten harms everyone's brain. Popular books and posts have spread this idea, often by stretching what is true for people with celiac disease to the whole population.

    Hype Meter: "Gluten damages everyone's brain"

    StrongModeratePreliminaryHype
    Verdict: Hype. The best available test in people without celiac disease, a 2021 study of 13,494 US women, found no difference in memory and thinking scores between women who ate the most gluten and those who ate the least, over decades of diet records. The authors concluded their results do not support cutting gluten to protect thinking in people without celiac disease or established gluten sensitivity. Nervous system problems are a real concern for people with celiac disease, not proof of harm for everyone. What would change the grade: Repeated randomized trials showing that people without celiac disease think better after cutting gluten than after a disguised placebo diet. No such evidence exists.

    Here is a closer look at that 2021 study by Wang, Lebwohl, and colleagues. It used the Nurses' Health Study II: women filled out food questionnaires from 1991 to 2015, then took computer-based tests of attention, memory, and overall thinking between 2014 and 2019. After adjusting for other factors, gluten intake was not linked to any of the three scores.

    How to read a gut-brain claim: gluten and thinking in 13,494 women

    Question This study
    Mouse or human? Human: 13,494 US women without celiac disease, average age about 61.
    Correlation or cause? Correlation (an observational cohort). It found no link, which is good evidence against a large harm, but it can't prove zero effect.
    How many? Large: 13,494 women. All were women, mostly middle-aged, so results may not apply to teens or men.
    How long? Diet tracked from 1991 to 2015 (about 24 years); thinking tested once, from 2014 to 2019.
    Who paid for it? US National Institutes of Health grants, plus foundation and research awards to some authors; the paper says funders had no role in the study's design or conclusions.
    Is something being sold? No product. Some authors reported personal fees from drug or nutrition companies for work outside this study, as disclosed in the paper.

    The careful conclusion: for people without celiac disease, there's no good evidence that eating gluten harms thinking, and decent evidence against a big effect. It doesn't prove nobody is sensitive to wheat, which is the next question.

    12.8 Non-Celiac Gluten Sensitivity and the Fructan Twist¶

    Some people without celiac disease or wheat allergy say they feel better when they stop eating wheat. Their symptoms, often bloating, belly pain, tiredness, or "fog," are real. The question is what causes them.

    Non-celiac gluten sensitivity (NCGS) is a condition in which people without celiac disease or wheat allergy report gut or other symptoms after eating gluten-containing foods that improve when they avoid them; unlike celiac disease, it doesn't damage the villi, and it has no biomarker. NIDDK notes that gluten sensitivity does not damage the small intestine. Because there is no blood test for NCGS, a 2015 group of experts (the Salerno criteria, Catassi and colleagues) proposed a standard test: a double-blind, placebo-controlled gluten challenge. In plain words, the person eats food that secretly contains gluten for a week and food that doesn't for another week, and neither the person nor the researchers know which is which until the end.

    When scientists run blinded tests like this, something surprising often happens.

    • In a 2013 study by Biesiekierski and colleagues, 37 adults with self-reported gluten sensitivity and IBS first ate a diet low in FODMAPs (fermentable carbohydrates you met in Chapter 10). Their symptoms improved. Then, when they were secretly given gluten or a placebo protein, symptoms got worse by similar amounts with both. Gluten-specific effects showed up in only 8% of participants.
    • In a 2017 meta-analysis of 11 gluten rechallenge studies, Lionetti and colleagues found that about 30% of people with suspected NCGS had symptoms return on gluten, but overall, relapse on gluten was not significantly different from relapse on placebo. In the studies that followed the stricter Salerno method, though, more people relapsed on gluten than on placebo (40% vs 24%), so a small group may truly react to gluten.
    • In a 2018 study by Skodje and colleagues in Norway, 59 people on a self-chosen gluten-free diet (with celiac disease ruled out) ate muesli bars containing gluten, fructan, or neither, for a week each, in random order, without knowing which. Symptom scores were highest with fructan, and gluten was no different from placebo.

    That last study introduces a new player. A fructan is a carbohydrate built from linked fructose (fruit sugar) units that the human small intestine can't break down; it is a type of FODMAP found in wheat and some other foods, and once it reaches the colon it can draw in water and increase gas, which can trigger symptoms in sensitive people. Wheat, barley, and rye contain both gluten (a protein) and fructans (carbohydrates). So when someone stops eating wheat and feels better, they've cut both at once, and the gluten-free label gets the credit.

    There's also the placebo and nocebo side. When people expect a food to make them sick, they often feel worse after eating it, even when it is secretly a placebo. That is visible in these trials, where symptoms rose with the dummy food too. This doesn't mean the symptoms are "fake." Expectation changes how the brain processes gut signals, a real gut-brain effect you studied in Chapter 10.

    Hype Meter: "Many people who think they're gluten sensitive are really reacting to fructans"

    StrongModeratePreliminaryHype
    Verdict: Preliminary. Blinded studies point away from gluten for many people: in one double-blind crossover trial of 59 adults, fructan raised symptom scores while gluten matched placebo, and an earlier trial of 37 adults found gluten-specific effects in only 8%. But these are small, short studies of adults, results differ between trials, and other wheat components may matter for some people. What would change the grade: Larger blinded trials in several countries, including teens, that test gluten, fructans, and other wheat parts separately and find the same pattern would raise it to Moderate.

    The honest summary: NCGS is debated, not disproven. The symptoms are real; the trigger is uncertain, and for many people it may not be gluten. Because celiac disease must be ruled out first, and because diet changes have costs, the right path for anyone who suspects a wheat problem starts with a doctor.

    12.9 Allergy, Intolerance, and Everything in Between¶

    People often use "allergy," "intolerance," and "sensitivity" as if they mean the same thing. They don't, and mixing them up can be dangerous in one direction and wasteful in the other.

    Food allergy: the immune system's alarm¶

    A food allergy is a harmful reaction caused by a specific immune response that happens reliably every time a person is exposed to a particular food. That definition comes from the 2010 US food allergy guidelines written by an expert panel sponsored by the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases (NIAID). The guidelines say most food allergies are driven by a particular antibody, though some are not.

    Immunoglobulin E (IgE) is a class of antibody that drives immediate allergic reactions; in food allergy, IgE made against a food protein sits on immune cells and triggers them to release chemicals within minutes when the food is eaten. Picture a smoke alarm wired directly to a sprinkler system: IgE is the hair-trigger sensor, and the immune cells it sits on (such as mast cells, which you met in earlier chapters) are the sprinklers. The NIAID guidelines describe upper gut symptoms of IgE food allergy appearing within minutes, and the most serious reaction, anaphylaxis, as a rapid, whole-body allergic reaction that can cause death. People with food allergies work with their doctors on an emergency plan.

    A wheat allergy is a food allergy, and it is different from celiac disease. NIDDK explains that in both, the immune system reacts to wheat, but wheat allergy can cause symptoms like itchy eyes or trouble breathing and does not cause long-term damage to the small intestine. The NIAID guidelines describe celiac disease as an immune reaction to food that does not involve IgE.

    Food intolerance: a processing problem¶

    A food intolerance is a reliable, unpleasant reaction to a food that does not involve the immune system, often because the body can't fully digest something. The NIAID guidelines use exactly this distinction: reactions without an immune mechanism are intolerances, not allergies.

    The classic example is lactose intolerance, digestive symptoms such as bloating, gas, and diarrhea after eating or drinking lactose (milk sugar), because the small intestine can't break down all of it. According to NIDDK, the problem underneath is lactose malabsorption: the small intestine makes low levels of lactase, the brush-border enzyme that breaks down lactose, and in many people lactase levels drop after infancy. Undigested lactose passes into the colon, where bacteria break it down and create fluid and gas.

    NIDDK estimates that about 68% of the world's population has lactose malabsorption, and about 36% of people in the United States, though not everyone with malabsorption has symptoms. NIDDK says it is most common in Africa and Asia and least common among people whose families come from Europe; in the United States it is more common among African American, American Indian, Asian American, and Hispanic or Latino people. Since most adults worldwide have it, lactose malabsorption is the typical adult human pattern, not a defect. Most people with lactose intolerance can handle some lactose.

    NIDDK draws the contrast clearly: lactose intolerance is a digestion problem, while milk allergy is an immune system disorder. Someone with lactose intolerance who drinks a milkshake may have a miserable afternoon. Someone with a milk allergy could have a life-threatening reaction.

    Use the right word

    Words matter here. Calling an intolerance an "allergy" at a restaurant is understandable. But when someone with celiac disease or a true allergy speaks up, staff need to know that trace amounts count. Being precise helps everyone get the right level of care.

    12.10 When Avoiding Food Backfires¶

    If gluten-free food is easy to buy, why not cut gluten (or dairy, or anything else) "just in case"? An unneeded restrictive diet is cutting out foods or whole food groups without a diagnosed medical reason, which can bring costs such as nutrient gaps, missed or delayed diagnoses, extra expense, and social stress, without a proven benefit. Here's what can go wrong:

    • A missed diagnosis. As Section 12.5 explained, going gluten-free before testing can make celiac tests falsely negative. Someone who actually has celiac disease may never get a clear answer, and might later return to gluten without knowing their intestine is being damaged.
    • Nutrition gaps. Whole food groups carry nutrients. NIDDK notes, for example, that people avoiding dairy need other sources of calcium and vitamin D. This is one reason NIDDK recommends that people with celiac disease work with a dietitian, and the same logic applies to anyone cutting out foods.
    • Cost and hassle. NIDDK notes that packaged gluten-free foods tend to cost more.
    • Social stress and worry. Constantly checking labels and avoiding shared meals can make eating feel stressful and isolating. For some people, focusing intensely on "bad" foods can turn into an unhealthy relationship with food. Food is not good or bad in a moral sense; it's a question of what a particular body needs.
    • Wrong conclusions. As the NCGS trials showed, feeling better after cutting a food doesn't prove that food was the problem. Expectation, other changes made at the same time (like eating fewer FODMAPs or less processed food), and natural ups and downs in symptoms can all play a part.

    None of this means food reactions are imaginary. It means the order matters: get checked first, while eating normally, and change your diet with professional help.

    For families

    If your teen has celiac disease, the biggest help is often practical: a gluten-free shelf, a separate toaster or cutting board, and relatives who understand that "just a little" isn't okay. Try not to describe foods as "good" or "bad," and avoid comments about weight; the goal is safety and health. If someone in the family wants to go gluten-free without a diagnosis, encourage a doctor visit first, because celiac disease runs in families and testing works best while still eating gluten. Ask the care team whether other relatives should be tested.

    At the Bedside: Tired, Pale, and Not Growing¶

    Meet the patient (fictional)

    Lucía is 14 and in ninth grade. For about a year she has felt tired all the time, even after a full night of sleep, and she gets belly pain and bloating several times a week. Her parents notice she hasn't grown much since last year while her friends shot up. Her school nurse sees that she looks pale and suggests a checkup. Lucía figures she's just stressed about starting high school.

    What the doctor checks: Her pediatrician reviews her growth chart (her height has fallen off her usual curve), asks about stools, diet, periods, and family history (no one in her family is known to have celiac disease, which doesn't rule it out, since many people with celiac disease are undiagnosed), and examines her belly and skin. Blood tests show iron-deficiency anemia. Because the symptoms could fit celiac disease, the doctor orders a tTG antibody blood test while Lucía is still eating her usual diet, and checks her total IgA level. Her tTG antibodies come back clearly high. She is referred to a pediatric gastroenterologist, who performs an upper endoscopy and takes a small bowel biopsy.

    Connecting the signals: Fatigue plus iron-deficiency anemia plus slow growth points to poor absorption, not just poor eating. The pathologist sees shortened, flattened villi and extra immune cells in the lining. With fewer villi, Lucía's small intestine has much less surface to absorb iron and other nutrients, which explains the anemia, the tiredness, and the slowed growth.

    Diagnosis: Celiac disease.

    Why it happens: Lucía carries a DQ2 or DQ8 variant (most people with celiac disease do), and when she eats gluten, modified gluten fragments trigger T cells in her intestine. The resulting inflammation causes villous atrophy, the same process you watched in the Villi Explorer, and her immune system makes antibodies against tTG, which the blood test detected.

    The mind-body side: At first Lucía is relieved to have an answer. Then the hard part starts: school lunches, sleepovers, and a family that bonds over home-cooked meals. She feels embarrassed asking friends to check ingredients, and anxious about getting sick at a party. Her care team names this social stress as part of the illness, not a side issue. A dietitian helps her plan safe options for school and events, the team screens her for anxiety and low mood at visits, and she joins a teen celiac support group online where other teens share practical tips. Her parents learn to set up the kitchen so she isn't the only one adjusting.

    Treatment today: A strict, lifelong gluten-free diet, taught by a registered dietitian, is the only proven treatment. Her team checks for and treats nutrient shortfalls such as low iron, monitors her growth, and rechecks her antibody levels over time. They also suggest that her close relatives talk to their doctors about testing.

    What's new in research: Scientists are testing medicines that might reduce damage from accidental gluten exposure. In a 2021 trial in the New England Journal of Medicine, Schuppan and colleagues gave 163 adults with well-controlled celiac disease either a placebo or an experimental pill (ZED1227) that blocks the tTG enzyme, while all of them ate a measured amount of gluten daily for 6 weeks. Biopsies showed less gluten-induced damage to the villi in the drug groups than in the placebo group. The trial was funded by the drug's developer. Evidence grade: Preliminary. It was a short, early trial in adults; the drug is experimental and is not an approved treatment, and it was tested as an add-on to the diet, not a replacement. As of September 2026, no medicine is FDA approved to treat celiac disease, though several are being tested in trials.

    Your turn: reason through a variation

    Lucía's older brother, 17, has no symptoms, but after her diagnosis he decides to stop eating gluten "to be safe." Two months later, his doctor orders a tTG antibody test, and it is normal. Does this show he doesn't have celiac disease?

    Answer: Not necessarily. tTG antibodies fall when gluten is removed, so a normal result after two months gluten-free could be a false negative. He might not have celiac disease, but the test can't tell well in this situation. His doctor might discuss options such as genetic testing for DQ2 and DQ8 (if negative, celiac disease is very unlikely) or a planned, supervised return to gluten before retesting. This is why testing should happen before a diet change.

    This is a fictional teaching case. It is not medical advice. If you or someone you know has symptoms like these, talk to a doctor.

    Common Misconceptions¶

    Myth: Celiac disease is a type of food allergy.
    Reality: Celiac disease is an immune disorder, but it doesn't involve IgE, the antibody behind classic food allergies, and it damages the villi over time instead of causing an immediate allergic reaction. Wheat allergy is a separate condition.

    Myth: If you think gluten bothers you, going gluten-free first will help the doctor figure it out.
    Reality: NIDDK says doctors don't recommend starting a gluten-free diet before testing, because removing gluten can make the blood test and biopsy look normal even in someone with celiac disease.

    Myth: A gluten-free diet is healthier for everyone.
    Reality: For people with celiac disease it's essential, but in people without celiac disease, a 2021 study of 13,494 women found no link between gluten intake and thinking scores. Cutting gluten without a reason can cost more, complicate diagnosis, and narrow the diet.

    Myth: Lactose intolerance is a milk allergy.
    Reality: Lactose intolerance is a digestion problem: the small intestine can't break down all the lactose. A milk allergy is an immune reaction to milk protein that can be dangerous. NIDDK estimates about 68% of the world's people have lactose malabsorption, so it's very common and usually not dangerous.

    Myth: If the genetic test shows DQ2 or DQ8, you have celiac disease.
    Reality: About 30% of people carry DQ2 or DQ8, and only about 3% of carriers develop celiac disease. A negative gene test helps rule celiac disease out; a positive one doesn't rule it in.

    Your Gut Check¶

    Test yourself. Try each one before you open the answer.

    1. Claim Check: "Gluten-free cured my brain fog. Gluten is inflammatory for EVERYONE, so ditch it today! Use my code for 20% off."

    Grade: Hype. One person feeling better isn't evidence of cause: expectation, other diet changes, and natural ups and downs could explain it, and blinded trials of self-reported gluten sensitivity often find gluten no worse than placebo. In people without celiac disease, a study of 13,494 women found no link between gluten intake and thinking scores. And there's a discount code, so someone profits from the claim. A careful version: "Gluten triggers real immune damage in people with celiac disease, about 1 in 100 people. If you think gluten affects you, get tested before cutting it out."

    2. Predict: Riya's orchestra friend Mei has celiac disease and has followed a strict gluten-free diet for a year. At the potluck, she eats a gluten-free brownie that was cut with the same knife as a regular brownie. Predict what might happen in her intestine, and why.

    The knife could transfer a small amount of gluten (cross-contact). In someone with celiac disease, even small amounts can restart the immune reaction: tTG-modified gluten fragments activate T cells, inflammation increases, and repeated exposures can shorten the villi again. One small exposure may or may not cause noticeable symptoms, but NIDDK notes symptoms can return with small amounts of gluten, which is why separate utensils matter.

    3. Explain: A 15-year-old with untreated celiac disease starts having more bloating and gas after drinking milk. Explain how celiac disease could cause this.

    Lactase, the enzyme that digests lactose, is part of the brush border on the microvilli of the small intestine. In untreated celiac disease, villous atrophy damages the lining and its brush border, so less lactase is available. Undigested lactose reaches the colon, where bacteria ferment it, producing gas and bloating. NIDDK lists lactose intolerance due to intestinal damage as a celiac symptom, and it often improves as the lining heals.

    4. Apply: Sam's friend Jordan (fictional) gets hives and a swollen lip within 10 minutes of eating a shrimp dish. Jordan's sister gets bloated and has diarrhea a few hours after eating ice cream. Classify each reaction and explain the difference.

    Jordan's reaction fits a food allergy, most likely IgE-mediated: it happened within minutes and involved the skin and lips, which is how IgE-triggered immune cells release chemicals fast. That kind of reaction can become serious, so Jordan should see a doctor about it. The sister's reaction fits a food intolerance such as lactose intolerance: a digestion problem without an immune attack, appearing hours later with gut symptoms. Only a doctor can confirm either, but the timing and symptoms point in different directions.

    5. Read the data. In the 2018 Skodje trial, 59 adults with self-reported gluten sensitivity (celiac disease ruled out) ate muesli bars containing gluten, fructan, or neither (placebo) for a week each, in random order, without knowing which. Symptoms were scored on a gut symptom scale where higher means worse. Here are the published results:

    Challenge Average overall symptom score Number of people whose worst week was this one
    Gluten 33.1 13
    Fructan 38.6 24
    Placebo 34.3 22
    5. What does this data show, and what can't it show?

    On average, symptoms were highest during the fructan week, and the gluten week was no worse than placebo (33.1 vs 34.3). Notice that 22 people had their worst week on placebo, which shows how strongly expectation and day-to-day variation affect symptoms. The data can't show that no one reacts to gluten, since averages can hide a few true responders, and it can't be generalized to teens or other countries from 59 adults in Norway tested for one week per food. A useful follow-up would be a larger blinded trial, including teens, that repeats each challenge more than once per person to see whether individual reactions are consistent.

    Exercises¶

    Remember¶

    1. Name the three grains that naturally contain gluten.
    Answer

    Wheat, barley, and rye.

    1. What are the two main tests used to diagnose celiac disease?
    Answer

    A tissue transglutaminase (tTG) antibody blood test, and a small bowel biopsy taken during an upper endoscopy to look for villous atrophy.

    Understand¶

    1. Explain why villous atrophy leads to poor absorption of nutrients.
    Answer

    Villi and their microvilli give the small intestine a huge surface for absorbing nutrients. When the villi shrink and flatten, there is much less surface touching digested food, so less iron, vitamins, and other nutrients get absorbed. That can cause anemia, fatigue, and slow growth.

    1. Explain the difference between a food allergy and a food intolerance, using one example of each.
    Answer

    A food allergy is a reproducible reaction caused by the immune system, often driven by IgE antibodies and appearing within minutes; a peanut or milk allergy is an example, and it can be life-threatening. A food intolerance doesn't involve the immune system; lactose intolerance, where the gut can't fully digest milk sugar, is an example, and it causes uncomfortable but not dangerous gut symptoms.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • Intestinal villi and their microvilli give the small intestine an enormous absorbing surface. In celiac disease, the immune reaction to gluten causes villous atrophy, which reduces absorption and can cause anemia, fatigue, and slow growth.
    • Celiac disease affects about 1 in 100 people worldwide (1.4% by blood test, 0.7% confirmed by biopsy) and about 2 million people in the US. It requires HLA-DQ2 or HLA-DQ8 gene variants plus gluten, but only about 3% of carriers develop it.
    • Diagnosis uses a tTG antibody blood test and, for most people, a small bowel biopsy. Testing must happen before going gluten-free, because the diet can make tests falsely negative.
    • A strict, lifelong gluten-free diet is the only proven treatment. Symptoms often improve in days to weeks, but the lining can take months to years to heal, so follow-up matters.
    • Celiac disease can affect the nervous system, including peripheral neuropathy and, less often, gluten ataxia (Moderate evidence). The claim that gluten harms everyone's brain is Hype.
    • Food allergy (often IgE-driven, fast, possibly dangerous), food intolerance (non-immune, like lactose intolerance), celiac disease, and non-celiac gluten sensitivity are different conditions. Blinded trials suggest fructans, not gluten, may trigger symptoms in many people with self-reported gluten sensitivity (Preliminary).
    • An unneeded restrictive diet can delay diagnosis, leave nutrient gaps, cost more, and add stress. Get checked first, then change your diet with professional help.
